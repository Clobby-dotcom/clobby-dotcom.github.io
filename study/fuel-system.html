    Options

    The simulated airplane is in level flight. If the engine quits, the propeller keeps windmilling, so the engine-driven pump keeps turning. RPM, EGT, and fuel flow are illustrative, tuned to match the POH's best-economy fuel flows at 55, 65, and 75% power. Always use the POH and the airplane's own gauges.

    How fuel gets to the engine

    Fuel takes one path from the selected tank to the cylinders. These are the parts it passes through, in order (POH 7.19, Figure 7-13):

    1. Wing tank. One 25-gallon tank in the leading edge of each wing, 24 gallons usable in each.
    2. Fuel selector valve. Connects the left or right tank to the engine, or shuts fuel off.
    3. Firewall. The supply line passes from the cabin into the engine compartment.
    4. Filter and drain. The fuel strainer. It catches dirt and water, and it has a quick drain at the lower front of the firewall.
    5. Electric fuel pump. The backup pump, run by the FUEL PUMP switch.
    6. Engine-driven fuel pump. The main pump. It runs whenever the engine turns.
    7. Fuel injector servo. Meters fuel in proportion to the air going into the engine. The throttle and mixture cables connect here.
    8. Fuel flow transducer. Measures the fuel flow and sends it to the G3X.
    9. Fuel distributor. Splits the fuel evenly among the four cylinders.
    10. Fuel nozzles. One per cylinder. Each sprays fuel into its cylinder's intake port.

    The two pumps sit one after the other in the same line. The POH schematic shows no return line, so all the fuel that leaves the selected tank goes to the engine.

    Why a pump has to be working all the time. The tanks are in a low wing, below the engine, so fuel can't get there by gravity the way it does in a Cessna. If neither pump is working, the engine gets no fuel.

    The fuel selector

    The selector has three positions: L, R, and OFF (POH 7.19, Figure 7-11). There's no BOTH position. The engine always feeds from one tank, so managing fuel means choosing a tank and switching to keep the tanks even.

    • To select OFF, you press the button on the selector and turn it counterclockwise. Turning clockwise takes you to L or R, and the button pops back out so you can't land on OFF by accident.
    • Use the tanks alternately in cruise to keep the airplane in lateral trim, and don't run a tank completely dry in flight (POH 4.5k).
    • To switch tanks, turn the electric fuel pump on first, switch, leave the pump on for a short time, then turn it off.
    • Before takeoff and landing, put the selector on the proper tank, normally the fullest one (POH 4.5h, 4.5m).
    • With the autopilot on, the fuel imbalance between tanks can't be more than 10 gallons. That's a POH limitation.

    The checklists put the selector to OFF for an engine fire during start (if the fire keeps going), for an engine fire in flight, and once you're committed to a power-off landing.

    Two fuel pumps

    The engine-driven pump is a diaphragm pump on the engine. It's the main pump, and because the engine turns it, it works whenever the propeller turns, even if the propeller is just windmilling after the engine quits. The electric fuel pump is there in case the engine-driven pump fails (POH 7.19). Its FUEL PUMP switch is on the switch panel to the left of the throttle quadrant.

    After an alternator failure, the POH limits fuel pump use to 2 minutes so the battery lasts about 30 minutes.

    Mixture and leaning

    The fuel injector servo measures the air going into the engine and meters fuel in proportion to it (POH 7.7). The throttle sets how much air goes in, and the servo sends fuel to match. The mixture lever sets the ratio of fuel to air. Full forward is full rich. Full aft is idle cutoff, where the servo sends no fuel at all. That's how you shut the engine down (POH 7.9).

    As you climb, the air gets thinner, and the same mixture setting runs richer than the engine needs. You lose some power and burn extra fuel. Leaning brings the ratio back.

    When to lean

    • Lean in cruise above 5,000 feet. Below that, you can lean when you're using 75% power or less (POH 4.5k).
    • If you aren't sure how much power you're using, keep the mixture full rich below 5,000 feet.
    • Go full rich for the descent and keep it full rich for landing, so the engine accelerates right away if you have to go around (POH 4.5l, 4.5m).
    • Set the mixture before takeoff. At a high-elevation field, you may need to lean it for smooth running (POH 4.13).

    How to lean

    1. Best economy: pull the mixture slowly aft until EGT peaks.
    2. Best power: lean to peak EGT, then push the mixture forward until EGT is 100°F rich of the peak.
    3. If the engine runs rough before EGT peaks, use the EGT where the roughness started as your peak.

    The G3X Engine page has a Lean Assist button that does the watching for you. It finds the first cylinder to peak, which is the one that counts, and shows how far each EGT is from its peak (POH 7.15).

    Fuel vapor on hot days

    During long idles in high temperatures, the fuel can form vapor bubbles in the lines (POH 4.13). You'll see the idle RPM and fuel flow wander, the engine respond poorly to the throttle or quit at idle, or the RPM drop more than 175 on the mag check. To clear it, run the engine at 1,800 to 2,000 RPM for 1 to 2 minutes and check that it idles smoothly. Then taxi with the mixture leaned and the power as high as practical, up to 1,200 RPM. Go back to full rich before takeoff and confirm the engine runs smoothly at full throttle before you start the takeoff roll.

    Starting the engine

    A fuel-injected engine is primed with the mixture, not a primer. With the electric pump on, pushing the mixture forward lets fuel through the servo to the nozzles, which spray it into the intake ports. A few seconds is enough. Then the mixture goes back to idle cutoff so the engine doesn't flood before it starts (POH 4.5d).

    The fuel steps in each start (POH 4.5d)
    StartThrottleFuel pumpMixture
    Cold (oil below 140°F)Open ½ inchONPrime 3 to 5 seconds, then idle cutoff. Engage the starter, then advance.
    Hot (oil 140°F or above)Open ½ inchONIdle cutoff, no prime. Advance when the engine starts.
    FloodedFull openOFFIdle cutoff. Advance when the engine starts, then pull the throttle back.

    Overpriming is the usual cause of an engine fire during start (POH 3.5a). If it happens, keep cranking to pull the fire into the engine, with the mixture at idle cutoff and the throttle open.

    What the G3X shows

    • Fuel quantity. A float in each tank sends its level to the GEA 24 engine interface, which feeds the display (POH 7.19). When either tank reads 3 gallons or less, you get a single chime and a flashing red fuel quantity (POH 3.5c).
    • Fuel flow. The flow transducer between the servo and the distributor measures what the engine is using. This airplane has no fuel pressure gauge, so fuel flow is the number to watch if you suspect a fuel problem.
    • Fuel used and remaining. The G3X counts fuel from the fuel flow, starting from what you enter on the Fuel Calc tab of the Engine page. The POH says not to use the fuel flow or the totalizer as your primary way of knowing what's in the tanks (POH 2.25). It only knows what you told it.
    • EGT. Your guide for leaning.

    Preflight

    • Look in the tanks. Fuel at the bottom of the tab in the filler neck means 17 gallons usable in that tank (POH 7.19).
    • Drain the tank sumps. Each tank has a quick drain at its lowest inboard rear corner. Check for water, sediment, and the right fuel: blue 100LL or green 100. Nothing lower is approved.
    • Drain the fuel strainer twice, once with the selector on each tank, so you sample the line from both (POH 8.21). Drain the tank sumps first.
    • Check that each drain closes completely and doesn't leak.
    • Check that the tank vents are clear.
    • Drain before the first flight of the day and after every refueling.

    If the fuel system has been completely drained, run the engine for at least 3 minutes at 1,000 RPM on each tank after refueling, so no air is left in the lines (POH 8.21).

    Test yourself

    Name, in order, the parts fuel passes through from the tank to the cylinders.

    Tank, fuel selector valve, firewall, filter and drain (strainer), electric fuel pump, engine-driven fuel pump, fuel injector servo, fuel flow transducer, fuel distributor, and the four fuel nozzles.

    Why does this airplane need a fuel pump working at all times?

    It's a low wing. The tanks are below the engine, so fuel can't reach it by gravity.

    When does the electric fuel pump go on?

    For engine start (except a flooded start), takeoff, approach and landing, and switching tanks. It's normally off in cruise.

    Why is the fuel pump normally off in cruise?

    So that if the engine-driven pump fails, you notice right away instead of having the electric pump quietly cover for it.

    How do you turn the fuel selector to OFF, and why is it built that way?

    Press the button on the selector and turn it counterclockwise. The button keeps you from turning to OFF by accident when you mean to switch tanks.

    Your engine quits in cruise and the fuel flow drops. What do you do first?

    Fly the airplane at 76 KIAS. Then fuel pump ON and the selector to the fullest tank. If a tank ran dry, give it up to 10 seconds for fuel to refill the lines.

    What does the mixture lever control on this fuel-injected engine?

    The ratio of fuel to air. The servo meters fuel to match the airflow, and the mixture sets how rich or lean that match is. Idle cutoff stops the fuel entirely.

    How do you lean for best economy? For best power?

    Best economy is at peak EGT. Best power is leaned to peak, then enriched until EGT is 100°F rich of the peak.

    When does the POH say to keep the mixture full rich?

    Below 5,000 feet if you aren't sure of the power setting, for descent, and for approach and landing.

    How do you prime this engine?

    Fuel pump ON, then the mixture forward for 3 to 5 seconds and back to idle cutoff. Skip the prime for a hot start.

    How much usable fuel is in a tank filled to the bottom of the tab?

    17 gallons. Full is 24 usable per tank, 48 total.

    Why do you drain the fuel strainer twice?

    Once with the selector on each tank, so you sample the fuel from both tanks' lines.

    After a long idle on a hot day, the engine idles rough and the fuel flow wanders. What's happening, and what do you do?

    Fuel vapor in the lines. Run at 1,800 to 2,000 RPM for 1 to 2 minutes, taxi with the mixture leaned at up to 1,200 RPM, and go full rich and check full power before takeoff.
