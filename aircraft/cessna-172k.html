Cessna 172K

Study reference only. The POH and the airplane's own records always win.

Specs and limits

The 172K was built for the 1969 and 1970 model years. FAA Type Certificate Data Sheet (TCDS) 3A12 lists serial numbers 17257162 through 17258486 as 1969 models and 17258487 through 17259223 as 1970 models. Cessna sold it in two versions, the Model 172 and the Skyhawk, which share the same limits but differ in standard equipment and empty weight.

This airplane predates the standardized Pilot's Operating Handbook. It came with an Owner's Manual, organized into seven sections: operating checklist, description and operating details, emergency procedures, operating limitations, care of the airplane, operational data, and optional systems. When this page says "POH," it means the Owner's Manual and any supplements or placards in your airplane. The figures here come from TCDS 3A12 and the 1970 Owner's Manual. A 1969 airplane has its own manual, so check it against these numbers.

Many 172Ks have been modified since they left the factory. Engine conversions, long-range tanks, and other changes made by Supplemental Type Certificate (STC) can change horsepower, fuel, weights, and speeds. If your airplane's records, flight manual supplements, or placards differ from anything here, they win.

In the normal category, spins and other acrobatic maneuvers are prohibited. In the utility category, the manual allows chandelles, lazy eights, and steep turns at entry speeds up to 122 MPH, plus spins and stalls entered with a slow deceleration. Flight into known icing is prohibited. With standard equipment the airplane is approved for day and night VFR, and with the right optional equipment for IFR.

V-speeds

The 172K's manual and airspeed indicator use miles per hour. The knots in the table below are conversions (MPH × 0.869, rounded) except the four limit speeds, where TCDS 3A12 prints the knots itself. Fly the numbers on your airplane's indicator. If someone has replaced it with a knots indicator, the POH supplement or placard for that change applies.

The manual lists the airspeed limits and stall speeds as calibrated airspeed (CAS). It doesn't say whether the normal operating speeds, like climb and approach, are indicated or calibrated, except for the short-field approach, which it gives as indicated. The airspeed correction table in Section VI shows the indicator reading well below calibrated airspeed at low speeds. For example, 50 MPH indicated with flaps up is 58 MPH calibrated. So near the stall, the needle will read lower than the stall speeds listed here.

The manual doesn't name a best glide speed in a performance chart. The 80 MPH figure is the glide it tells you to set up after an engine stoppage. The airspeed indicator markings in Section IV are a white arc from 52 to 100 MPH, a green arc from 59 to 140 MPH, a yellow arc from 140 to 174 MPH, and a red line at 174 MPH. Systems

Engine and induction

The O-320-E2D is a four-cylinder, carbureted engine with a manual primer. Carburetor heat is a push-pull knob. Expect to use it. An unexplained RPM drop in cruise is the manual's sign of carb ice, and it calls for carb heat before any big power reduction. On the ground, keep carb heat off, because heated air bypasses the air filter. Some airplanes have an optional carburetor air temperature gauge with a yellow arc from −15°C to 5°C for the icing range.

Fuel

Two wing tanks feed the engine by gravity. There's no fuel pump. The selector has LEFT, RIGHT, BOTH, and OFF. Use BOTH for takeoff, climb, landing, and long slips or skids. LEFT or RIGHT is for cruise. Fuel passes through a strainer on its way to the carburetor, and each tank has a sump drain. On BOTH in cruise, the tanks can feed unevenly if you don't hold the wings level.

This model surprises students with a fuel vapor issue. The TCDS requires a placard near the selector on airplanes through serial 17258855, unless a Cessna fuel kit has been installed, telling you to switch to a single tank once you're cruising above 5,000 ft. The 1970 manual explains why. Volatile fuel, heat, and altitude can form vapor and cause rough running. If that happens on one tank, switch to the other. If it happens on BOTH, go to one tank for a minute and then to the other. Check your airplane's placard to see whether it applies.

Electrical

The 172K has a 14-volt system with an engine-driven alternator and a 12-volt battery on the firewall. The master switch is split. The BAT half controls everything, and the ALT half controls the alternator. An ammeter shows charge or discharge, not total load. A split bus cuts power to the avionics side while you're starting or using external power. Circuits are protected by push-to-reset breakers. The TCDS lists a few early 1969 serial numbers (17258105 through 17258112) as 28-volt airplanes, so check yours.

Flaps

The flaps are electric, with a switch and a position indicator on the panel. They travel from 0° to 40°. Section II of the manual says 30° and 40° are never recommended for takeoff, and to avoid slips with more than 30° of flaps, because the nose can pitch down in some combinations of airspeed, sideslip, and CG.

Landing gear and brakes

The gear is fixed tricycle with a steerable nosewheel and a shimmy damper. The brakes are hydraulic, and there's a parking brake handle on the panel. Normal tire pressures in the servicing table are 24 psi for the mains and 26 psi for the nose. Your airplane's tire sizes may differ, so check the servicing page in its manual.

Pitot-static and gyros

Airplanes with attitude and heading indicators run them on a vacuum system. The runup check expects 4.6 to 5.4 inches of mercury on the suction gauge. The turn coordinator or turn-and-bank indicator is electric. An alternate static source valve was optional. The manual says using it shifts airspeed by about 2 MPH and altitude by about 15 ft at most. Pitot heat was optional too.

Avionics

The Owner's Manual shows radios, a transponder, an autopilot, and the marker beacon lights as optional equipment. Its radio selector panel has positions for two COMs, two NAVs, an ADF, and DME, along with the speaker and phone switches.

Installed avionics vary by airplane. The panel in each fleet airplane will be added here by your instructor. Until then, use the supplements in that airplane's manual for any radio, GPS, or autopilot it carries.

Weight and balance

The datum is the lower front face of the firewall. Arms are measured in inches aft of it, so anything ahead of the firewall, like the engine oil, has a negative arm. The leveling reference is the upper doorsill.

Procedures and flows

These are summaries for study. Use the checklist in your airplane, and the Owner's Manual sections cited, when you fly.

Engine start (Section I and Section II)

Set the fuel selector to BOTH and turn off radios and electrical equipment. Mixture rich, carb heat cold, prime as needed for the temperature, throttle open about 1/8 inch. A warm engine needs no prime. After the start, check that oil pressure comes up within 30 seconds, or about twice that in very cold weather. If it doesn't, shut down. The manual has separate cold-weather starting procedures in Section II.

Runup (Section I and Section II)

Run up at 1,700 RPM. The magneto drop should be no more than 125 RPM on either side, with no more than 50 RPM between them. Check suction at 4.6 to 5.4 inches of mercury on airplanes with vacuum gyros. To check the alternator before a night or instrument flight, add a brief electrical load, like the flaps or landing light, and the ammeter should stay within a needle width of zero. Early in the takeoff roll, confirm the engine is turning about 2,260 to 2,360 RPM at full throttle. Above 5,000 ft field elevation, lean for maximum RPM before takeoff.

Takeoff and landing flaps (Section II)

  • Normal and obstacle takeoffs use flaps up.
  • 10° shortens the ground roll by about 10 percent, but you lose that gain by the time you clear a 50 ft obstacle. Use it for soft or rough fields.
  • Normal landings can use any flap setting. Short-field landings use 40°.
  • On a go-around, add full power and bring the flaps to 20° right away. Retract the rest once you're clear of obstacles and accelerating.

Emergency memory items (Section III)

  • Engine failure in flight. Set up an 80 MPH flaps-up glide. If there's time, check fuel quantity, the fuel selector, mixture, primer locked, and the ignition switch.
  • Forced landing. Mixture to cutoff, fuel selector off, switches off except the master, then the master off and doors unlatched before final. Touch down slightly tail-low.
  • Engine fire in flight. Mixture to cutoff, fuel selector off, master off, cabin heat off, and a 120 MPH glide to a forced landing. Don't try a restart.
  • Electrical fire. Master off and vents closed. If you need electrical power, turn things back on one at a time to find the bad circuit.
  • Alternator failure, shown by a steady discharge on the ammeter. Turn off the alternator side of the master and nonessential equipment, and land as soon as practical.
  • Rough engine. Try carb heat. Check each magneto. Adjust the mixture.
  • Inadvertent icing. Pitot heat on if installed, turn back or change altitude, defrost on, carb heat as needed, keep flaps up, and land at the nearest airport.

Spin recovery for the utility category is in Section IV. The manual's version is opposite rudder, then forward pressure on the control wheel, then a smooth pull-out once rotation stops.
