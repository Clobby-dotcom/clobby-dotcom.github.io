Weight and balance

How to check that the airplane isn't too heavy and that the weight is in the right place.

Why it matters

Too heavy

  • Longer takeoff roll and a slower, shallower climb
  • Higher stall speed and higher approach and landing speeds
  • Longer landing roll and more load on the structure

CG too far forward

  • Higher stall speed, because the tail has to push down harder to hold the nose up
  • You may run out of elevator to rotate on takeoff or to flare on landing

CG too far aft

  • Less stable in pitch, with lighter controls that are easy to over-control
  • Stall and spin recovery get harder, and far enough aft may not be possible

The terms

Datum
The reference point every distance is measured from. The POH says where it is.
Arm (or station)
The distance in inches from the datum to an item. Behind the datum is positive.
Moment
Weight times arm, in pound-inches. Some POHs divide it by 100 or 1,000 and call it a moment index.
Center of gravity (CG)
The point where the airplane would balance, given as an arm.
CG envelope
The allowed range of CG at each weight, drawn as a graph in the POH.
Basic empty weight
The airplane with its installed equipment, unusable fuel, and full oil. It comes from that airplane's own weight and balance record. Some older POHs use licensed empty weight, which leaves out the oil.
Useful load
Maximum weight minus basic empty weight: people, bags, and usable fuel.
CG = total moment ÷ total weight

You're taking two passengers and a bag on a three-hour flight with 40 gallons of fuel. Is it legal?

  1. Start with the airplane's empty weight and moment

    Here it's 1,650 lb at an arm of 39.0 in, for a moment of 64,350 lb-in.

  2. Weigh everything you're adding

    Use real weights for people and bags. 100LL weighs 6 lb per gallon, so 40 gallons is 240 lb.

  3. Multiply each weight by its arm

    Takeoff loading
    ItemWeight (lb)Arm (in)Moment (lb-in)
    Basic empty weight1,65039.064,350
    Pilot18037.06,660
    Front passenger16037.05,920
    Rear passenger15073.010,950
    Baggage4095.03,800
    Fuel, 40 gal24048.011,520
    Total2,420103,200

  4. Check the weight

    2,420 lb is under the 2,550 lb maximum.

  5. Find the CG

    103,200 ÷ 2,420 = 42.6 in.

  6. Check the CG against the envelope

    At 2,420 lb the limits are about 39.7 in and 47.3 in. 42.6 is between them.

    Takeoff: 2,420 lb at 42.6 in. Within limits.

  7. Do it again for landing

    Three hours at 8 gallons per hour, with taxi and climb, burns about 24 gallons, or 144 lb. Take that weight and its moment back out:

    Landing
    ItemWeight (lb)Arm (in)Moment (lb-in)
    Takeoff total2,42042.6103,200
    Fuel burned, 24 gal−14448.0−6,912
    Landing total2,27642.396,288

    96,288 ÷ 2,276 = 42.3 in. The CG moved forward because the fuel sits aft of it.

    Landing: 2,276 lb at 42.3 in. Within limits.

Moving weight around

If the CG is out of limits, you can move something instead of leaving it behind:

CG change = weight moved × distance moved ÷ total weight

Moving the 40 lb bag from the baggage area (95.0 in) to the rear seat (73.0 in) is 22 inches forward. 40 × 22 ÷ 2,420 = 0.36 in, so the takeoff CG goes from 42.6 to about 42.3 in.
