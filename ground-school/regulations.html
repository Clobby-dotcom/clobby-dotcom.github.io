Regulations

As pilot in command, you're responsible for knowing and following the rules. Here are the parts of the Federal Aviation Regulations you'll use on almost every flight, and what to do if something goes wrong.

How the rules are organized

The Federal Aviation Regulations are Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (14 CFR). They're divided into parts, and a private pilot works mostly with three of them:

Part 1
Definitions, such as exactly what "night" or "operate" means.
Part 61
Pilot certification: getting a certificate, what it lets you do, and staying current.
Part 91
General operating and flight rules: how you fly.

Accident reporting is in a different title: 49 CFR Part 830, written by the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB). Airworthiness, inspections, and required equipment are covered in the lesson on documents, inspections, and equipment.

"14 CFR 91.155" means Part 91, section 155.

Certificates and medical

What to carry

When you act as pilot in command, you must have with you your pilot certificate, a government-issued photo ID such as a driver's license, and a medical certificate or the documents that show you meet BasicMed (14 CFR 61.3). Show them to the FAA, NTSB, TSA, or a law enforcement officer who asks.

As a student pilot, you also carry your logbook with your instructor's endorsements on every solo cross-country flight (14 CFR 61.51).

Medical certificates

A private pilot needs at least a third-class medical certificate, or must meet the BasicMed requirements (14 CFR 61.23). A third-class medical lasts until the end of the 60th calendar month after the exam if you were under 40 on the day of the exam, or until the end of the 24th calendar month if you were 40 or older.

"Calendar month" means the certificate runs to the last day of that month.

A medical certificate is not the whole story. You may not act as pilot in command while you know or have reason to know of a medical condition that would make you unable to meet the requirements for your medical (14 CFR 61.53). Before every flight, a quick check is the IMSAFE checklist:

  • Illness: any symptoms?
  • Medication: any prescription or over-the-counter drugs that could affect you?
  • Stress: anything at work or home weighing on you?
  • Alcohol: any drink within the last 8 hours, or 24 to be safer?
  • Fatigue: are you rested?
  • Emotion (some versions say eating): are you upset, and have you eaten and had water?

BasicMed

BasicMed lets you fly without an FAA medical certificate if you've held one at some point after July 14, 2006, hold a valid U.S. driver's license, take an online medical course every 24 calendar months, and get a checkup from a state-licensed physician every 48 months (14 CFR 61.23, Part 68). It limits altitude, speed, airplane size, and the number of people on board (14 CFR 61.113(i)).

Change of address

If you move, you have 30 days to tell the FAA your new permanent address. After that, you can't exercise the privileges of your certificate until you do (14 CFR 61.60).

Staying current

Flight review

To act as pilot in command, you must have completed a flight review within the previous 24 calendar months (14 CFR 61.56). It's at least 1 hour of flight training and 1 hour of ground training with an instructor, who then endorses your logbook. Passing a checkride for a new certificate or rating also counts.

Carrying passengers

To carry passengers, you must have made 3 takeoffs and 3 landings within the preceding 90 days, as the sole manipulator of the controls, in an aircraft of the same category and class (and type, if a type rating is required) (14 CFR 61.57(a)). In a tailwheel airplane, the landings must be to a full stop.

To carry passengers at night, the 3 takeoffs and 3 landings must be to a full stop, during the period from 1 hour after sunset to 1 hour before sunrise (14 CFR 61.57(b)).

Three different "nights"

Additional endorsements

Some airplanes need a one-time logbook endorsement from an instructor before you can act as pilot in command (14 CFR 61.31):

  • A complex airplane, which has retractable landing gear, flaps, and a controllable-pitch propeller.
  • A high-performance airplane, which has an engine of more than 200 horsepower.
  • A tailwheel airplane.

What a private pilot may do

A private pilot may not act as pilot in command of an aircraft carrying passengers or property for compensation or hire, or for compensation or hire at all (14 CFR 61.113). The FAA reads "compensation" broadly, and it can include things like logged flight time or goodwill.

The main exceptions in 61.113 are:

  • You may share the operating expenses of a flight with your passengers, but you must pay at least your pro rata share. Only fuel, oil, airport expenditures, and rental fees count as shareable costs.
  • You may fly for business if the flight is only incidental to that business and you don't carry passengers or property for compensation.
  • A few narrow exceptions, such as certain charitable flights, have their own conditions.

If someone offers to pay you for a flight, assume it isn't allowed until you've confirmed otherwise.

Operating rules

Pilot in command responsibility

The pilot in command is directly responsible for, and is the final authority on, the operation of the aircraft (14 CFR 91.3). In an in-flight emergency, you may deviate from any rule as far as needed to handle it, and the FAA can ask you for a written report afterward.

You may not operate an aircraft in a careless or reckless manner that endangers life or property (14 CFR 91.13).

Preflight action

Before each flight, you must become familiar with all available information about it (14 CFR 91.103). Runway lengths and takeoff and landing distances are required for every flight. The rest of the list applies to flights away from the vicinity of an airport. A common mnemonic is NWKRAFT:

N
NOTAMs
W
Weather reports and forecasts
K
Known ATC traffic delays
R
Runway lengths at the airports you'll use
A
Alternatives available if the flight can't be completed as planned
F
Fuel requirements
T
Takeoff and landing distance data for the airplane

The rule doesn't name NOTAMs, but "all available information" covers them.

Alcohol and drugs

You may not act as a crewmember within 8 hours after drinking alcohol, while under its influence, with a blood alcohol concentration of 0.04 percent or more, or while using any drug that affects your faculties in a way contrary to safety (14 CFR 91.17). Any alcohol-related or drug-related motor vehicle action, such as a DUI conviction or a license suspension, must be reported to the FAA's Security and Investigations Division within 60 days (14 CFR 61.15).

Fuel

Under VFR, you may not begin a flight unless you have enough fuel to fly to your first point of intended landing and then fly 30 more minutes at normal cruise by day, or 45 more minutes at night (14 CFR 91.151). These are legal minimums. Many pilots set a personal minimum of an hour of fuel at landing.

Minimum safe altitudes

Except when taking off or landing (14 CFR 91.119):

  • Anywhere, fly high enough that if the engine quits you can make an emergency landing without undue hazard to people or property on the surface.
  • Over a congested area of a city, town, or settlement, or over an open-air assembly of people, stay at least 1,000 feet above the highest obstacle within 2,000 feet horizontally of the airplane.
  • Over other areas, stay at least 500 feet above the surface. Over open water or sparsely populated areas, you may go lower but must stay at least 500 feet from any person, vessel, vehicle, or structure.

VFR cruising altitudes

When you cruise more than 3,000 feet above the surface and below 18,000 feet MSL, use these altitudes based on your magnetic course (14 CFR 91.159):

  • Magnetic course 0 through 179 degrees: odd thousands plus 500 feet, such as 3,500 or 5,500.
  • Magnetic course 180 through 359 degrees: even thousands plus 500 feet, such as 4,500 or 6,500.

It's magnetic course, not heading, that counts.

Right-of-way

Having the right-of-way never excuses you from seeing and avoiding other aircraft (14 CFR 91.113).

  • An aircraft in distress has the right-of-way over all other traffic.
  • When aircraft of the same category are converging at about the same altitude, the one on the right has the right-of-way.
  • When aircraft of different categories converge, a balloon has the right-of-way over everything, a glider over an airship or airplane, and an airship over an airplane. An aircraft towing or refueling another has the right-of-way over all other engine-driven aircraft.
  • When two aircraft approach head-on, each turns to the right.
  • An aircraft being overtaken has the right-of-way. The overtaking aircraft passes well clear to the right.
  • An aircraft on final or landing has the right-of-way over aircraft in flight or on the ground. When two aircraft are approaching to land, the lower one has the right-of-way, but it may not use that to cut in front of another aircraft on final or to overtake it.

Passengers and oxygen

  • Before takeoff, you must brief each passenger on how to fasten and unfasten their seat belt and shoulder harness, if installed, and tell them to fasten it. Each person must be belted in for taxi, takeoff, and landing, with a narrow exception for children under 2 held by an adult (14 CFR 91.107).
  • Above 12,500 feet MSL up to and including 14,000 feet MSL, the required crew must use supplemental oxygen for any time there beyond 30 minutes. Above 14,000 feet, the crew must use it the whole time. Above 15,000 feet, every occupant must be provided with oxygen (14 CFR 91.211).
  • If anyone other than a crewmember is aboard, you may not exceed 60 degrees of bank or 30 degrees nose-up or nose-down pitch unless each occupant wears an approved parachute. Flight tests and training for a certificate or rating with an instructor are exempt for required maneuvers such as spins (14 CFR 91.307).

Accidents and incidents

49 CFR Part 830 tells you when you must notify the NTSB and what to do with the wreckage.

Aircraft accident
An event, from the time anyone boards with intent to fly until everyone has left the aircraft, in which any person suffers death or serious injury, or the aircraft receives substantial damage (49 CFR 830.2).
Fatal injury
An injury that results in death within 30 days of the accident.
Serious injury
Includes hospitalization for more than 48 hours starting within 7 days of the injury, any broken bone except simple fractures of fingers, toes, or nose, internal organ injury, and second- or third-degree burns or any burns over more than 5 percent of the body.
Substantial damage
Damage that hurts the structural strength, performance, or flight characteristics and would normally need major repair or replacement of the part. Damage limited to an engine, bent cowling, dented skin, small punctures, or damage to landing gear, tires, flaps, brakes, or wingtips doesn't count.

When to notify the NTSB immediately

The operator must notify the nearest NTSB office immediately after any accident, and after any of these incidents (49 CFR 830.5). The ones most likely to apply to a private pilot are:

  • Flight control system malfunction or failure
  • A required flight crewmember unable to perform normal duties because of injury or illness
  • In-flight fire
  • Aircraft collision in flight
  • Damage to property other than the aircraft estimated to exceed $25,000 to repair (or its fair market value if destroyed, whichever is less)
  • Release of all or part of a propeller blade, unless caused only by ground contact
  • An overdue aircraft believed to have been in an accident

The full list also covers turbine engines, large multiengine airplanes, helicopters, airline operations, and electronic flight displays.

Wreckage and reports

Preserve the wreckage, cargo, and flight recorders until the NTSB takes custody or releases them. You may move them only to remove people, protect the wreckage from further damage, or protect the public from injury, and if you do, record their positions if you can (49 CFR 830.10).

After an accident, the operator files a written report (NTSB Form 6120.1) within 10 days. If an overdue aircraft is still missing, file it within 7 days. After an incident, file a report only if the NTSB asks for one (49 CFR 830.15).

Notification is an immediate call. The written report comes later, and only for accidents unless the NTSB asks.
