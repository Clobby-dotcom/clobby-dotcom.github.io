Ground school

Cross-country planning

A cross-country flight uses almost everything else in ground school: charts, weather, performance, weight and balance, and decision-making. Planning one well on the ground is what makes the flight itself calm.

What the rules require

Before any flight, the pilot in command must become familiar with all available information concerning that flight. For a flight under IFR or a flight not in the vicinity of an airport, that has to include weather reports and forecasts, fuel requirements, alternatives available if the planned flight can't be completed, and any known traffic delays ATC has told you about. For every flight, it includes the runway lengths at the airports of intended use and the takeoff and landing distance information for your airplane (14 CFR 91.103).

Many students remember these with the mnemonic NWKRAFT:

N, NOTAMs
Not named in the rule, but part of "all available information," and examiners expect you to check them: closed runways, unlit towers, temporary flight restrictions.
W, weather
Reports and forecasts for departure, route, and destination.
K, known ATC delays
Any delays ATC has advised you of.
R, runway lengths
At every airport you plan to use.
A, alternatives
Where you'll go if you can't complete the flight as planned.
F, fuel requirements
Enough for the trip plus the required reserve.
T, takeoff and landing distances

What counts as a cross-country

For the private pilot certificate, cross-country time that counts toward your aeronautical experience requires a landing at a point more than 50 nautical miles in a straight line from the original departure point (14 CFR 61.1). Your training will include at least 3 hours of dual cross-country and 5 hours of solo cross-country. One solo cross-country must cover at least 150 nautical miles total, with full-stop landings at three points and one segment of at least 50 nautical miles in a straight line between the takeoff and landing points (14 CFR 61.109).

As a student pilot, you need a cross-country endorsement from your instructor, and your instructor must review your planning and endorse your logbook before each solo cross-country flight (14 CFR 61.93).

Route and altitude

Start on the sectional chart. Draw a line from departure to destination and look along it for anything that makes the straight line a bad idea:

  • Airspace: Class B, C, and D areas, restricted and prohibited areas, MOAs, and any TFRs from the NOTAMs.
  • Terrain and obstacles: the maximum elevation figure (MEF) in each quadrangle shows the height you need to clear the highest terrain or obstacle in that box.
  • Landing options: a route that passes airports along the way gives you places to go if the engine, the weather, or your passenger's stomach turns against you.

It's often better to bend the route a little so it follows good landmarks, avoids a large lake or remote area, or stays near airports. A VOR or a chain of airports can also make a good route.

Then pick checkpoints along the route, usually every 10 to 20 nautical miles. Good checkpoints are easy to identify from the air: a town with a distinct shape, a highway crossing a river, a lake, or an airport. Avoid ones that look like everything around them. Your first checkpoint should be close to the departure airport so you can confirm right away that you're on course. For how to read the chart and use these features, see Navigation.

Cruising altitude

When you're in level cruise more than 3,000 feet above the surface, VFR cruising altitudes are set by your magnetic course (14 CFR 91.159):

The rule uses magnetic course, which is the direction of your path over the ground. Your heading may differ because of wind. Within those choices, weigh the winds aloft, the cloud bases, terrain, airspace floors and ceilings, and how far you can glide if the engine quits. Higher is usually better for gliding range and radio reception, if the clouds and winds allow it.

The nav log

The nav log turns your line on the chart into headings, times, and fuel for each leg. It's dead reckoning on paper: if you hold the planned heading for the planned time, you should be over the next checkpoint. Work each leg the same way:

  1. Measure the true course with a plotter, against a meridian near the middle of the leg.
  2. Measure the distance in nautical miles.
  3. Get the true airspeed for your planned altitude and power setting from the POH.
  4. Get the winds aloft forecast for your altitude. Winds aloft are given in true direction, so they work directly with the true course.
  5. Use an E6B or electronic flight computer to find the wind correction angle (WCA) and ground speed.
  6. Apply the corrections to get from true course to compass heading.
  7. Divide distance by ground speed for the time, and multiply time by fuel burn for the fuel.
TC ± WCA = TH ± variation = MH ± deviation = CH

True course (TC) plus or minus the wind correction gives true heading (TH). Correct for wind toward the side the wind is coming from: add for a wind from the right, subtract for a wind from the left. Magnetic variation, from the isogonic line on the sectional, gives magnetic heading (MH). Remember "east is least, west is best": subtract easterly variation, add westerly variation. Finally, the compass deviation card in the airplane gives compass heading (CH).

Fuel for the leg is the time multiplied by the cruise fuel burn from the POH. Add fuel for engine start, taxi, run-up, and the climb to cruise altitude, which uses more fuel per minute than cruise. The POH has a time, fuel, and distance to climb chart for that.

Leave blank columns on the nav log for the actual time over each checkpoint and the actual ground speed. Filling those in during the flight is how you find out whether the forecast winds were right and whether your fuel plan still holds.

Fuel, weather, and performance

Fuel

For a VFR flight, you may not begin the flight unless, considering wind and forecast weather, there is enough fuel to fly to the first point of intended landing and, assuming normal cruising speed, to fly after that for at least 30 minutes during the day or 45 minutes at night (14 CFR 91.151).

That's the legal minimum, and it leaves little room. Forecast winds are often wrong, fuel gauges in light airplanes are not very accurate, and a diversion or a busy airport can add time. Many instructors and flight schools set a personal minimum of an hour of reserve. Visually check the fuel quantity during preflight, and know how much is usable in that airplane from the POH.

Weather

Get a full picture of the weather for the departure, route, destination, and alternates, for the whole time you'll be flying plus a margin. Check the current reports, the forecasts, the winds aloft for your nav log, and any advisories such as AIRMETs and SIGMETs. Compare what you find to your personal minimums. Then get an official briefing from Flight Service or an approved online source, which also creates a record that you were briefed. Look at the trend: improving weather and deteriorating weather with the same numbers right now are very different flights. For the products and how to read them, see Weather services.

Performance and weight and balance

Run the numbers for the actual conditions at each airport you plan to use. On a hot day at a high-elevation airport, takeoff distance and climb rate can be much worse than at home. Compare the takeoff and landing distances to the runway lengths, and leave a good margin. See Performance.

Compute weight and balance for the actual load, at takeoff and at landing. A cross-country often means a passenger, baggage, and full fuel, the combination most likely to put you over gross weight. If you're over, you'll have to leave something behind, which may mean carrying less fuel and adding a fuel stop. See Weight and balance.

Flight plans and flight following

A VFR flight plan isn't required for most flights, but it's strongly recommended for a cross-country. It's how someone knows to come looking for you. You file it with Flight Service, by phone or online, and then you have to open it, usually by radio with Flight Service after takeoff, or through an online account. Filing alone doesn't activate it.

When you land, close the flight plan with Flight Service. If you don't close it, search and rescue procedures start. Under the AIM, if a VFR flight plan isn't closed within 30 minutes after the estimated arrival time, Flight Service begins trying to find you, starting with phone calls and expanding from there. If you're going to be late, update your arrival time with Flight Service.

ATC doesn't watch over a VFR flight plan. For that, request VFR flight following, also called radar traffic advisories, from approach or center. They'll give you a transponder code and call out traffic near you, workload permitting. Flight following also means a controller already knows who and where you are if something goes wrong. You still have to see and avoid other traffic.

En route, diversions, and getting lost

On departure, note your takeoff time, then the time you level off and set cruise power. Turn to the first heading, find the first checkpoint, and confirm you're on course. At each checkpoint, write down the actual time, compare it to the plan, and work out the actual ground speed. If you're consistently left or right of course, adjust the heading. If you're slower than planned, update the arrival time and fuel.

Use pilotage, the matching of what you see on the ground to the chart, together with dead reckoning, holding the planned heading and time. A GPS is a great backup and a good source of situational awareness, but you still need to be able to follow your progress on the chart if it fails or you lose the signal.

Diverting

You might divert because of weather, a sick passenger, a fuel problem, or a mechanical issue. The PHAK's guidance is to make the decision early and turn toward the diversion airport promptly. Then work out the details:

  • Pick an airport you can reach easily. A chart in a moving airplane is hard to measure, so estimate the course with a straightedge or your plotter, and use a rough wind correction.
  • Estimate the distance and time with a rule of thumb, then refine it with the flight computer or GPS once you're established.
  • Check your fuel against the new time en route.
  • Tell someone. Update your flight plan with Flight Service or tell ATC.

If you're lost

Getting lost happens. The danger comes from wandering, burning fuel, and flying into worse weather while trying to figure it out. A common memory aid is the four Cs:

  • Climb, if weather allows, to see farther and improve radio and radar coverage.
  • Communicate. Call ATC or Flight Service, or use the emergency frequency 121.5 MHz if you need to.
  • Confess that you're lost or unsure of your position. Controllers would much rather help early.
  • Comply with the instructions you get.

Meanwhile, hold a constant heading, so you're not wandering, and look for a large landmark: a big highway, a river, a lake, or a town you can identify. A VOR radial or a GPS can also fix your position fast. If you're truly in trouble, such as low on fuel or in deteriorating weather, declare an emergency and set your transponder to 7700. Landing at the nearest airport and figuring it out on the ground is always an option.
