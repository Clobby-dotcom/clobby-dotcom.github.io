Ground school

Airport operations

Most collisions and near misses happen on or near airports, where everyone is low, slow, and busy. Knowing the pattern, the paint, the signs, and the radio calls lets you fit into the flow and keep track of everyone else.

The traffic pattern

A traffic pattern is a standard path around the runway, so every pilot knows where to look for other airplanes. At a nontowered airport, it's the main thing keeping traffic apart.

The legs

Departure leg
The climb straight out along the runway after takeoff.
Crosswind leg
A short leg flown at a right angle to the runway, after the departure leg.
Downwind leg
Parallel to the runway, flown in the opposite direction from landing. This is where most pilots join the pattern and run the before-landing checklist.
Base leg
At a right angle to the runway, between downwind and final. You start your descent planning here.
Final approach
The path straight in to the runway along its extended centerline.

Direction and altitude

Turns in the pattern are to the left unless the airport says otherwise (14 CFR 91.126). A right-hand pattern is shown in the Chart Supplement, on the sectional chart next to the airport ("RP" followed by the runways), and on the ground by traffic pattern indicators next to the segmented circle.

The FAA recommends a pattern altitude of 1,000 feet above ground level for propeller airplanes, unless the airport publishes a different one (AIM 4-3-3, AC 90-66). Check the Chart Supplement for each airport you use. Remember that pattern altitude is above the airport, so add field elevation to get the number on your altimeter.

Entering and leaving

  • The recommended entry is a 45 degree angle to the downwind leg, joining abeam the middle of the runway at pattern altitude.
  • Get down to pattern altitude before you join. Descending into the pattern from above puts you where other pilots aren't looking.
  • If you arrive on the wrong side of the field, cross well above pattern altitude, then descend and come around to the 45 degree entry. AC 90-66 also describes a midfield crossing at pattern altitude to join downwind, used where the airport's procedures allow it.
  • To leave, continue straight out or turn 45 degrees toward the pattern side after passing the departure end of the runway and reaching pattern altitude.
  • If you fly a straight-in approach at a nontowered airport, announce it early and give way to traffic already in the pattern.

Reading the wind on the ground

With no tower, you pick the runway. Use the AWOS or ASOS if there is one, and confirm with the wind sock, which points downwind. Listen to the CTAF too: if the wind is calm and everyone is using one runway, join them.

Runway and taxiway markings

Runway markings are white. Taxiway markings and holding position markings are yellow. That one rule tells you a lot about where you are.

Runway number
The runway's magnetic direction, rounded to the nearest 10 degrees with the last zero dropped. Runway 27 points about 270 degrees magnetic. Parallel runways add L, C, or R.
Centerline, threshold, and aiming point
A dashed white centerline runs the length of the runway. White stripes across the start mark the threshold, and on many runways two broad white bars farther down give you an aiming point.
Displaced threshold
White arrows on the centerline lead up to a white bar across the runway. The area with the arrows can be used for taxi, takeoff, and the landing rollout from the other direction. You can't land on it in the arrows' direction.
Blast pad or stopway
Yellow chevrons. Not usable for taxi, takeoff, or landing.
Closed runway or taxiway
A yellow X on the pavement. Also check NOTAMs, since temporary closures are not always painted.
Taxiway centerline
A solid yellow line. Keep your nose wheel on it for obstacle clearance. Near a runway, many airports add dashed yellow lines on each side of the centerline (an enhanced centerline) to warn that a hold short line is coming.
Runway holding position marking
Four yellow lines across the taxiway, two solid and two dashed. If you're on the solid side, you stop there and need a clearance (or, at a nontowered airport, a clear look and a radio call) to go across. Coming off the runway, you're clear of it only once your whole airplane has crossed the holding position marking.
ILS critical area holding position
A yellow ladder across the taxiway. When the tower tells you to hold short of the ILS critical area, stop here so your airplane doesn't bend the localizer or glide slope signal.
Non-movement area boundary
A solid and a dashed yellow line, solid on the non-movement side (usually the ramp). At a towered airport, ground control doesn't control the ramp, so you need a clearance before crossing onto the movement area.

Airport signs

The color of a sign tells you what kind of information it gives before you can read it.

A runway holding position sign shows both runway numbers in the order you'd see them. "15-33" with 15 on the left means runway 15 is to your left.

Before you taxi at an unfamiliar airport, pull up the airport diagram and the Chart Supplement, trace your route, and note any hot spots. A hot spot is a place where pilots have had trouble before, often a confusing intersection or a spot where taxiways cross runways close together.

Airport lighting

Rotating beacon

The beacon helps you find the airport at night or in poor visibility. A civilian land airport flashes alternating white and green. A military airport shows two quick white flashes between green flashes. If the beacon is on during the day at an airport in Class B, C, D, or E surface area airspace, the weather may be below VFR minimums, but the beacon is not a reliable sign of the weather. Check the reports.

Runway and taxiway lights

  • Runway edge lights are white. On instrument runways, the last 2,000 feet (or half the runway, if less) show yellow toward the end as a caution zone.
  • Threshold lights show green to an airplane landing, and red from the other side to mark the runway end.
  • Taxiway edge lights are blue. Taxiway centerline lights, where installed, are green.

Visual glide slope indicators

These lights show whether you're above, on, or below a safe approach path. If one is installed, stay on or above it until you're close enough that a lower altitude is needed to land (14 CFR 91.129 states this for Class D, and it's good practice anywhere).

VASI
Two rows of lights. Red over white means you're on the glide path. White over white is high, and red over red is low. The VASI path gives obstacle clearance within 10 degrees either side of the runway centerline, out to 4 nautical miles from the threshold.
PAPI
One row of four lights. Two white and two red means on glide path. Three or four white means high, and three or four red means low.

Pilot-controlled lighting

At many nontowered airports, you turn the lights on at night by keying your microphone on a published frequency, usually the CTAF. Seven clicks within five seconds turns on the highest intensity, five clicks gives medium, and three gives low, where the system has more than one setting. The lights stay on for 15 minutes from the last time they're activated, so key them again on final if it's been a while. The Chart Supplement lists the frequency and which lights are pilot-controlled.

Talking on the radio

Nontowered airports

At an airport without an operating tower, pilots announce their own position and intentions on the common traffic advisory frequency (CTAF). The CTAF may be a UNICOM frequency, a MULTICOM frequency (122.9), or a tower frequency when the tower is closed. Find it on the sectional chart (a C in a dark circle marks it) or in the Chart Supplement.

Each call starts and ends with the airport name, because many airports share the same frequency: "Brownsville traffic, Cessna 1234X, ten miles south, inbound for landing, runway 31, Brownsville." The AIM recommends calls at these points:

  • About 10 miles out when inbound
  • Entering downwind, base, and final
  • Leaving the runway
  • Before taxiing, and before taxiing onto the runway for departure

Radios aren't required at most nontowered airports, so keep looking outside.

Towered airports

Listen to ATIS before you call, and tell the controller you have it by its letter. Then work through the frequencies: clearance delivery if the airport has it, ground for taxi, tower for takeoff and landing. You need a clearance from ATC to operate on a runway or taxiway, take off, or land at an airport with an operating control tower (14 CFR 91.129). You also need a clearance for each runway you cross, and you must read back every hold short instruction.

A useful order for any call is who you're calling, who you are, where you are, and what you want. If you don't understand an instruction, ask.

Light gun signals

If your radio fails at a towered airport, set your transponder to 7600 and watch the tower for light signals (14 CFR 91.125). Acknowledge in the air by rocking your wings by day, or on the ground by moving your ailerons or rudder. At night, flash your landing light or position lights.

Wake turbulence and runway incursions

Wake turbulence

Every wing that makes lift leaves rotating vortices behind its wingtips. They're strongest behind an airplane that is heavy, clean (gear and flaps up), and slow, as a large airplane is on takeoff and approach. The vortices sink below the flight path and drift with the wind. On the ground they spread outward. A light crosswind can hold the upwind vortex over the runway, and a light quartering tailwind can push vortices forward into the touchdown zone.

  • Landing behind a larger airplane, stay at or above its approach path and touch down past the point where it touched down.
  • Taking off behind a larger airplane, lift off before the point where it rotated and climb above its path, or wait.
  • At a towered airport you can ask for extra spacing.

Runway incursions

A runway incursion is any time an airplane, vehicle, or person is on a runway where it shouldn't be. Most come from pilots who lose track of where they are or misunderstand a clearance. A few habits prevent most of them:

  • Have the airport diagram out and follow your route on it while you taxi.
  • Write down taxi instructions and read them back.
  • Treat every hold short line as a stop sign, and look both ways down the runway before you cross, even with a clearance.
  • At night, turn on your landing light when you enter a runway so other pilots can see you.
  • If you're unsure where you are, stop and ask. At a towered airport, ask ground for progressive taxi instructions.

Student pilots should not accept a land and hold short (LAHSO) clearance. The AIM says students and pilots unfamiliar with LAHSO should not take part, and any pilot can decline one.
