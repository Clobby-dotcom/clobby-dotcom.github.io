Ground school

Navigation

Cross-country flying means knowing where you are, where you're going, and how long it will take. These are the tools a private pilot uses to answer those questions, from a paper chart and a watch to VOR and GPS.

Sectional charts

The sectional aeronautical chart is the main chart for VFR flying. It shows terrain, towns, roads, rivers, airports, airspace, navigation aids, and obstructions. Each sectional covers a large region and is printed at a scale of 1:500,000, which works out to about 7 nautical miles per inch. Sectionals are revised every 56 days, and an out-of-date chart can show the wrong frequencies, airspace, or obstacles. Check the effective date before every cross-country.

Latitude and longitude

Lines of latitude run east and west and measure how far north or south of the equator you are. Lines of longitude run north and south and measure how far east or west of the prime meridian you are. Both are given in degrees and minutes. One minute of latitude equals one nautical mile, so the tick marks on any line of longitude double as a distance scale.

Reading the chart

Airport symbols
Airports with a control tower are printed in blue. Airports without one are printed in magenta. The data block next to each airport gives its name, elevation, lighting, longest runway length, and frequencies such as the tower or CTAF.
Terrain and obstructions
Contour lines and color shading show the shape and height of the ground. Obstructions such as towers show their height above mean sea level in bold and their height above ground in parentheses.
Maximum elevation figure (MEF)
The large number in each quadrangle bounded by latitude and longitude lines. It's in thousands and hundreds of feet MSL, so 35 means 3,500 feet. It gives the height of the highest terrain or obstruction in that block plus a margin. It's a quick way to see how high you need to be to clear everything nearby.
Isogonic lines
Dashed magenta lines labeled with the magnetic variation, such as 3°W. You'll use them when you convert a true course to a magnetic one.

Pilotage and dead reckoning

Pilotage is navigating by looking outside and matching what you see to the chart. Dead reckoning is navigating by computing a heading, groundspeed, and time from known information such as the wind. On a real cross-country you use both together. Dead reckoning tells you where you should be at a given time, and pilotage confirms whether you're there.

Choosing checkpoints

Good checkpoints are easy to spot from the air and hard to confuse with something else. A town with a distinctive highway interchange, a large lake, a power plant, or a river bend all work well. A single small pond in an area full of ponds does not. Pick checkpoints every 10 to 20 miles or so, and choose some that sit a little off your course line so you can see them out the side window instead of under the nose.

Look at the big features first. A road, railroad, or shoreline that runs along your route can serve as a handrail. A large feature that crosses your route can confirm your progress even if your exact position is off by a mile or two.

Time, speed, and distance

Most of dead reckoning comes down to one relationship:

Distance = groundspeed × time

If your groundspeed is 120 knots, you cover 2 nautical miles each minute. If a leg is 30 nautical miles long, it will take 15 minutes. A flight computer such as the E6B, or an electronic version, does this math and the fuel math quickly. Fuel used is fuel burn per hour times time, and the burn rate for your airplane comes from its POH.

The navigation log

The nav log is where you organize all of this before the flight. For each leg it holds the checkpoint, course, altitude, wind, heading, distance, groundspeed, estimated time, and fuel. In flight you fill in the actual times. When an actual time differs from the estimate, the difference tells you the real groundspeed and lets you update your arrival time and fuel reserve.

Choosing an altitude

When you cruise more than 3,000 feet above the ground, the VFR cruising altitude rule applies. On a magnetic course of 0° through 179°, fly an odd thousand feet MSL plus 500, such as 5,500. On a magnetic course of 180° through 359°, fly an even thousand plus 500, such as 6,500 (14 CFR 91.159). The rule is based on magnetic course, not heading, so a big wind correction doesn't change which altitude you use. Also think about terrain, airspace, winds aloft, cloud clearance, and how far you'll be able to glide.

From true course to compass heading

You measure your course on the chart with a plotter, relative to true north, because the latitude and longitude lines run to true north. The compass doesn't point there, and the wind pushes you sideways. A few corrections turn the line on the chart into the number you'll hold on the heading indicator.

True course (TC)
The direction of the course line, measured from true north at about the middle of the leg.
Wind correction angle (WCA)
How many degrees you turn into the wind to stay on course. Wind from the right means a correction to the right, which adds degrees. Wind from the left subtracts. True course plus or minus the WCA gives the true heading.
Variation
The angle between true north and magnetic north where you are, read from the nearest isogonic line. Add west variation and subtract east variation to get magnetic heading. The memory aid is "east is least, west is best."
Deviation
The error in your particular compass caused by the airplane's own magnetic fields. It's printed on the compass correction card in the airplane. Applying it gives you compass heading.
TC ± WCA = TH ± variation = MH ± deviation = CH

Get the winds from the winds and temperatures aloft forecast for your planned altitude. That forecast gives wind direction relative to true north, which is why you apply the wind correction before variation. If your first checkpoint shows you drifting, adjust the heading and keep the new one.

VOR navigation

A VOR (VHF omnidirectional range) is a ground station that transmits signals your VOR receiver uses to tell you which radial you're on. A radial is a magnetic bearing going outward from the station, so there are 360 of them. VORs transmit between 108.0 and 117.95 MHz, and the frequency and Morse code identifier for each one are printed in its compass rose on the sectional.

Tuning and identifying

Before you use a VOR, tune it and listen to the Morse code identifier to confirm it matches the chart. If there's no identifier, the station may be under maintenance and its signal may not be reliable, even if the needle looks normal. Don't use it.

Reading the indicator

The course deviation indicator (CDI) has three parts:

  • The omni bearing selector (OBS), the knob you turn to select a course.
  • The needle, which shows whether the selected course is to your left or right. On a typical VOR display, full deflection is 10° or more off course and each of the five dots is 2°.
  • The TO/FROM flag, which shows whether the selected course would take you toward the station or away from it.

The VOR indicator doesn't know which way the airplane is pointed. It only shows where you are relative to the selected course. If you set the course to roughly match your heading, the needle points toward the course: needle right, fly right. If you fly with the OBS set opposite to your heading, the needle works backward. That's called reverse sensing, and it's the most common VOR mistake.

Tracking to and from a station

  1. Tune and identify the station.
  2. Turn the OBS until the needle centers with a TO flag. The number under the index is the course directly to the station.
  3. Turn to that heading and see which way the needle drifts. Correct toward the needle, then hold a heading that keeps it centered. That heading includes your wind correction.
  4. As you pass over the station, the flag flips from TO to FROM. To fly outbound on a radial, set that radial in the OBS and track it with a FROM flag.

You can find your position by tuning two VORs and noting which radial you're on from each. Where those radials cross on the chart is where you are.

Checking the receiver

For IFR flight, the VOR receiver has to have been checked within the preceding 30 days. The allowed error is ±4° on a VOT test signal or a designated ground checkpoint, ±6° on a designated airborne checkpoint, and 4° between two receivers checked against each other (14 CFR 91.171). The rule doesn't apply to VFR flight, but the knowledge test asks about it, and the same checks tell you whether you can trust the receiver.

The FAA is shutting down some VORs and keeping the rest as a backup network for GPS outages. Check the chart and NOTAMs for the stations you plan to use.

GPS

The Global Positioning System is a network of satellites that broadcast precise timing signals. A receiver works out its position from the signals of several satellites at once. Receivers check their own accuracy through a process called receiver autonomous integrity monitoring (RAIM), which needs five satellites in view, or four plus input from the altimeter. Many newer receivers also use WAAS, a ground-based correction system that makes the position more accurate and reliable.

GPS is the most accurate navigation tool you'll use, and it's easy to lean on it too much. Some habits that keep it working for you:

  • Keep the database current and know whether your unit is approved for the use you're making of it. A handheld or tablet is a backup for VFR situational awareness.
  • Enter the flight plan and check it against your nav log before takeoff. A single wrong identifier can send the magenta line somewhere you didn't plan.
  • Keep following along on the chart. If the GPS fails or loses its signal, you need to know where you are right then.

Diversions and lost procedures

Diverting

You might need to divert because of weather, a sick passenger, low fuel, or a mechanical problem. Make the decision early, while you still have options. Then:

  1. Turn toward the new destination right away, using a rough heading. You can refine it once you're headed the right way.
  2. Estimate the course and distance on the chart. Your thumb, a pencil, or the plotter works. Your GPS can give you a direct course too.
  3. Work out the time and fuel needed, and check that you can make it with a reserve.
  4. Check the new route for airspace, terrain, and obstructions, and tell someone. Update a VFR flight plan with Flight Service or ATC.

When you're lost

Being unsure of your position is uncomfortable, and it's easy to make it worse by wandering. A common memory aid is the five Cs:

  • Climb, to see farther and improve radio range, as long as you stay clear of clouds and airspace.
  • Communicate with ATC or Flight Service. Tell them you need help. Radar facilities can often locate you.
  • Confess that you're lost. Controllers would rather help early than late.
  • Comply with the help you're given.
  • Conserve fuel by reducing power to an efficient setting while you sort it out.

While you work the problem, hold a steady heading instead of circling. Look for large features such as a lake, a highway, or a city, and compare them to the chart near where you think you are. A VOR cross-check or your GPS can confirm your position quickly. If you're in real distress, use 121.5 MHz or squawk 7700.

The best defense against getting lost is the nav log. If you check off each checkpoint and write down the time, you'll notice a problem within minutes instead of half an hour later.
