Aeromedical factors

Your body was built for life on the ground, and flight puts it in conditions it handles badly: thin air, no visible horizon, and motion your inner ear can't read. Knowing how those conditions affect you lets you spot the problem early, before it affects how you fly.

Fitness to fly

To act as pilot in command or as a required crewmember, you need a valid medical certificate or, in some cases, to meet the BasicMed rules (14 CFR 61.23). Most student and private pilots hold a third-class medical. For a third-class medical, the certificate lasts 60 calendar months if you were under 40 on the date of the exam, and 24 calendar months if you were 40 or older (14 CFR 61.23).

Holding a medical certificate only says you were healthy on the day of the exam. Before every flight, the regulations put the decision on you. You may not act as pilot in command while you know or have reason to know of a medical condition that would make you unable to meet the requirements of your medical certificate, or while taking medication or getting treatment for a condition that would make you unable to meet them (14 CFR 61.53).

The IMSAFE checklist is the standard way to run that self-check before you leave for the airport:

I, illness
Do you have any symptoms? A cold that seems minor on the ground can block your ears and sinuses on descent.
M, medication
Have you taken any prescription or over-the-counter drugs? Many common ones, such as antihistamines and sleep aids, cause drowsiness or slow your reactions.
S, stress
Pressure from work, money, or family takes up attention you need for flying.
A, alcohol
Have you had a drink recently? The legal limit is 8 hours, and many pilots use 24. More on this below.
F, fatigue
Did you sleep well? Fatigue degrades judgment, and the tired pilot is usually the last to notice.
E, emotion
Are you upset by an argument, a loss, or bad news? Strong emotion narrows attention and invites risky choices.

Some older materials use "eating" for the E. Both are worth checking. Dehydration and low blood sugar cause headaches, fatigue, and poor concentration, and a long flight in a hot cockpit makes both worse. Bring water.

Other common problems to know for the test and for real life:

  • Middle ear and sinus blocks happen when air can't equalize through a congested passage. They hurt most on descent. Don't fly with a cold, and if a block starts, try swallowing, yawning, or gently pressing air into your ears with your mouth and nose closed (the Valsalva maneuver).
  • Motion sickness brings sweating, nausea, and loss of appetite. Open the fresh air vents, loosen tight clothing, look outside at a point on the horizon, and avoid unnecessary head movement. Many motion sickness drugs cause drowsiness, so check before using any of them.

Hypoxia and hyperventilation

Hypoxia means the body isn't getting enough oxygen. The PHAK sorts it into four types by cause:

Hypoxic hypoxia
Not enough oxygen is available to the lungs. This is the altitude kind: the air at altitude holds the same percentage of oxygen, but the lower pressure pushes less of it into your blood.
Hypemic hypoxia
The blood can't carry enough oxygen. Causes include anemia, recent blood donation, and carbon monoxide, which takes the place of oxygen on the red blood cells.
Stagnant hypoxia
The blood isn't flowing well enough. High G-loads, shock, or a cold limb can cause it.
Histotoxic hypoxia
The cells can't use the oxygen they receive. Alcohol and some drugs cause it.

Hypoxia is dangerous because it comes on quietly and often brings a sense of well-being (euphoria). Symptoms include headache, drowsiness, dizziness, tingling, slowed reactions, impaired judgment, and a bluish color at the lips and fingernails (cyanosis). Each person's symptoms differ, but yours tend to repeat, so learning your own early signs helps. Your time of useful consciousness shrinks fast as altitude increases.

The oxygen rules for most general aviation flights are in 14 CFR 91.211:

Supplemental oxygen requirements (14 CFR 91.211)
Cabin pressure altitudeRequirement
Above 12,500 ft MSL up to and including 14,000 ft MSLRequired flight crew must use oxygen for any part of the flight at those altitudes that lasts more than 30 minutes
Above 14,000 ft MSLRequired flight crew must use oxygen the entire time
Above 15,000 ft MSLEach occupant must be provided with oxygen

The regulations set the legal floor, and the effects start lower. The AIM notes that night vision can start to deteriorate at a cabin altitude as low as 5,000 feet, and it encourages pilots to use oxygen above 10,000 feet in the day and above 5,000 feet at night.

If you suspect hypoxia, use supplemental oxygen if you have it and descend to a lower altitude.

Hyperventilation

Hyperventilation is breathing too fast or too deep, usually from stress, fear, or anxiety. It blows off too much carbon dioxide and causes lightheadedness, tingling, visual changes, muscle spasms, and in time unconsciousness. Many of its symptoms look like hypoxia. The fix is to slow your breathing on purpose: breathe at a normal rate, talk out loud, or breathe into a paper bag. If you're at an altitude where hypoxia is possible and you can't tell which one you have, treat it as hypoxia first: use oxygen if you have it and descend.

Carbon monoxide

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a colorless, odorless gas in engine exhaust. In many piston airplanes, cabin heat comes from air warmed by passing around the exhaust system through a heater shroud. A crack in the exhaust can leak CO into that air and into the cabin. Because CO binds to hemoglobin far more readily than oxygen does, even a small amount builds up in your blood and causes hypemic hypoxia, and it takes a long time to clear.

Symptoms include headache, blurred vision, dizziness, drowsiness, and loss of muscle power. Because it's odorless, the smell of exhaust is a warning but its absence proves nothing. A CO detector in the cabin is cheap and worth having.

If you suspect CO:

  1. Turn off the cabin heat.
  2. Open the fresh air vents and windows if the POH allows it.
  3. Use supplemental oxygen if you have it.
  4. Land at the nearest suitable airport and get medical help. Have the exhaust system inspected before the airplane flies again.

Alcohol, drugs, and diving

Alcohol affects judgment, coordination, and vision, and its effects get stronger at altitude. Even small amounts impair you, and a hangover still impairs you after the alcohol is gone. Under 14 CFR 91.17, no one may act or attempt to act as a crewmember of a civil aircraft:

  • within 8 hours after drinking any alcoholic beverage,
  • while under the influence of alcohol,
  • while using any drug that affects their faculties in any way contrary to safety, or
  • while having a blood alcohol concentration of 0.04 or greater.

Except in an emergency, you also may not allow a person who appears to be intoxicated, or under the influence of drugs, to be carried in the aircraft. A medical patient under proper care is an exception (14 CFR 91.17).

The 8 hours is a minimum. Many pilots wait at least 24 hours after drinking, and longer after heavy drinking.

For medications, the safest approach is simple: if a drug's label warns about drowsiness or operating machinery, don't fly while it's in your system. Both prescription and over-the-counter drugs can be disqualifying, and so can the condition you're treating. When in doubt, ask an aviation medical examiner (AME) before you fly, not after.

Scuba diving

Diving loads your body with extra nitrogen. Flying soon after, at lower pressure, can let that nitrogen come out of solution and cause decompression sickness (the bends). The AIM recommends waiting at least 12 hours after a dive that did not need a controlled ascent before flying up to 8,000 feet, and at least 24 hours after a dive that did need one. Before flying above 8,000 feet, wait at least 24 hours after any dive.

Spatial disorientation and illusions

On the ground, you know which way is up from three sources: your eyes, the vestibular system in your inner ear, and the nerves in your skin, muscles, and joints. In flight, only your eyes can be trusted. The inner ear's semicircular canals sense rotation by the movement of fluid, and they stop sensing a steady turn once the fluid catches up with the canal walls. The other organs (the otoliths) can't tell gravity from acceleration. When you can't see the horizon, these sensors give you wrong answers that feel completely real.

The leans
After a slow roll into a bank that your inner ear didn't detect, a quick roll back to level makes you feel banked the other way. You tend to lean, or roll back into the original bank.
Coriolis illusion
Moving your head sharply during a prolonged constant-rate turn stimulates more than one canal at once and produces a strong sensation of tumbling. It's considered the most overwhelming of the vestibular illusions. Keep head movements slow, especially when reaching for something in a turn.
Graveyard spiral
In a long coordinated turn, the sense of turning fades. The pilot sees altitude decreasing and pulls back, which only tightens the spiral.
Graveyard spin
After recovering from a spin, the pilot feels a spin in the opposite direction and may re-enter the original spin trying to correct it.
Somatogravic illusion
Rapid acceleration, such as on takeoff, feels like a nose-up pitch, so the pilot may push the nose down. Rapid deceleration feels like nose-down, and the pilot may pull up.
Inversion illusion
An abrupt change from climb to straight and level can feel like tumbling backward. The pilot may push the nose down sharply.
Elevator illusion
An updraft that carries the airplane upward feels like a climb, and the pilot may push the nose down. A downdraft has the opposite effect.

The defense is to keep visual references and, when you lose them, to believe your instruments over your body. For a VFR pilot, the most important defense is to stay out of clouds and low visibility in the first place. Continued VFR flight into instrument conditions is a leading cause of fatal accidents, and disorientation is often how it ends.

Visual and landing illusions

  • A false horizon happens when sloping cloud tops, or ground lights blending with stars, make you level the wings on the wrong reference.
  • Autokinesis happens at night: stare at a single light against a dark background for several seconds and it seems to move. Keep your eyes moving.
  • A runway narrower than you're used to makes you feel too high, so you tend to fly a low approach. A wider runway does the reverse.
  • An upsloping runway or terrain makes you feel too high, so you tend to fly a low approach. A downslope does the reverse.
  • Featureless terrain, such as water, snow, or a dark area at night (the "black hole" approach), makes you feel higher than you are, so you tend to fly low.
  • Rain on the windscreen can make you feel higher than you are. Haze makes the runway look farther away. In both cases the tendency is to fly a low approach.

Most of these illusions push you low, toward the ground short of the runway. A visual approach slope indicator (VASI or PAPI) gives you a check that doesn't depend on your eyes' judgment of the picture. Use it when there is one.

Vision and night flying

Your eye has two kinds of light receptors. Cones, packed at the center of the retina, see color and fine detail but need good light. Rods, around the edges, work in dim light but don't see color or detail. At night, the cones are of little use, so a dim object can disappear if you look straight at it. Look slightly to the side of it instead (off-center viewing). Every eye also has a blind spot where the optic nerve connects.

Rods take time to adapt to darkness, about 30 minutes for full adaptation. Exposure to bright light, even briefly, undoes it. Before a night flight, avoid bright lights, and use the dimmest cockpit lighting that lets you read. If a bright light is unavoidable, close one eye to keep its adaptation.

Scanning for traffic

Your eyes only see detail in a narrow central area, and they need a moment to focus. Sweeping smoothly across the windscreen sees very little. The AIM recommends a series of short, regularly spaced eye movements, each bringing a new area of about 10 degrees into view, and pausing at each for at least about 1 second. Remember that an airplane on a collision course shows little or no movement against the windscreen: it just grows, slowly at first and then very fast.

Empty-field myopia is the eye's tendency to focus at a short distance when there's nothing to look at, such as in haze. Glance at a distant object to reset your focus.
