Ground school

Flight controls

Every control surface works the same way: it changes the shape of an airfoil, which changes the lift on that part of the airplane. Knowing what each one really does helps you fly coordinated, trim well, and handle crosswinds on the ground.

Three axes, three controls

An airplane moves around three axes, and all three pass through its center of gravity. Each primary flight control works mainly on one of them.

The primary flight controls

LateralWingtip to wingtipPitchElevator or stabilatorPush or pull the yoke or stick

In most light airplanes, cables, pulleys, pushrods, and bellcranks connect the cockpit controls to the surfaces.

Controls get more effective as airspeed rises, because more air flows past each surface. At high speed, small inputs make big changes. At low speed, the controls feel soft and need larger movements to get the same result. That mushy feel is one of the signs you're getting slow and close to a stall.

The controls work relative to the airplane. Pulling back pitches the nose up toward the top of the airplane, even when you're in a steep bank. That's why back pressure in a steep turn tightens the turn as well as holding the nose up.

Ailerons and adverse yaw

The ailerons are on the outer trailing edge of each wing, and they move in opposite directions. Turn the yoke left and the left aileron goes up while the right aileron goes down. The lowered aileron on the right wing adds camber and lift. The raised aileron on the left wing reduces lift. The difference rolls the airplane left.

Once you reach the bank you want, you neutralize the ailerons. In a normal turn, the airplane holds the bank on its own. In a steep turn, you may need a little opposite aileron to stop the overbanking tendency.

Adverse yaw

The wing with the lowered aileron makes more lift, and more lift means more induced drag. That extra drag pulls its wing back and yaws the nose away from the direction of the turn. Roll left without rudder and the nose first swings right. This is adverse yaw. It's strongest at low airspeed and with large aileron deflections, when the wing is already at a high angle of attack.

You correct it with rudder in the direction of the turn, applied at the same time as aileron. When you roll out, you use rudder in the direction of the roll-out. Once you're established in the turn, you need little or no rudder. Watch the ball in the inclinometer and "step on the ball": if it's off to the left, add left rudder.

Designers reduce adverse yaw several ways:

  • Differential ailerons, where the up aileron moves farther than the down aileron, adding drag on the side that's going down.
  • Frise-type ailerons, where the nose of the raised aileron sticks down into the airflow under the wing and adds drag on that side.
  • Coupled ailerons and rudder, linked by springs so the rudder moves with the ailerons.

None of these removes the need for you to coordinate with your feet.

Slips and skids

When the rudder and ailerons don't match, the airplane isn't moving straight through the air. In a slip, there's too much bank for the rate of turn and the ball falls to the inside of the turn. In a skid, there's too much rudder for the bank, the rate of turn is too high, and the ball moves to the outside. A skidding turn close to the ground is the setup for a stall-spin accident, so treat a skid as something to fix right away. A slip, used on purpose, is a useful tool for losing altitude and for crosswind landings.

Elevator, stabilator, and rudder

Elevator

The elevator is hinged to the back of the horizontal stabilizer. Pull back and the elevator moves up. That reduces lift on the tail (or increases its downforce), so the tail goes down and the nose goes up. Push forward and the opposite happens.

The key idea is that the elevator controls angle of attack. Pulling back raises the angle of attack. Whether the airplane climbs, slows down, or just tightens a turn depends on power and what the airplane is doing. In stalls, slow flight, and landings, the elevator is your angle of attack control.

T-tails

On a T-tail, the horizontal tail sits on top of the vertical fin, up out of the propeller slipstream and most of the wing's downwash. Pitch changes less when you add flaps or power. The trade-off is that the elevator has less airflow at low speed, so it can feel less effective during the takeoff roll and needs a higher speed before the nose comes up.

Stabilator

Some airplanes use a stabilator, a one-piece horizontal tail that pivots as a unit. It's very powerful, which would make it easy to over-control. To prevent this, it has an antiservo tab on its trailing edge. The tab moves in the same direction as the stabilator's trailing edge, which makes the control feel heavier as you move it further. The antiservo tab usually doubles as the trim tab.

Rudder

The rudder is hinged to the back of the vertical stabilizer. Push the left pedal and the rudder moves left. Air pushes on it and swings the tail right, so the nose yaws left. Banking is what turns the airplane. You use the rudder to keep the tail following the nose: correcting adverse yaw, countering left-turning tendencies during takeoff and climb, and holding alignment with the runway in a crosswind.

Like the other controls, the rudder works better with more airflow. At low speed with high power, the propeller slipstream over the tail helps. During the takeoff roll, you'll need more right rudder early and less as speed builds.

Flaps and other secondary controls

Secondary controls change the wing's shape or the control forces. They include flaps, leading edge devices, spoilers, and trim systems.

Flaps

Flaps are hinged surfaces on the inboard trailing edge of the wings. Extending them increases camber, and with some designs wing area too. The result is more lift and more drag at a given airspeed. That lets you:

  • Fly a steeper approach without picking up speed.
  • Approach and touch down at a slower speed, because the stall speed with flaps extended is lower.
  • Use partial flaps for short-field or soft-field takeoffs, if the POH calls for it.

Common flap types:

Plain flap
The simplest: a hinged section of the trailing edge. It adds lift but a lot of drag at larger settings.
Split flap
Only the lower surface deflects down. It makes slightly more lift than a plain flap and much more drag.
Slotted flap
A gap opens between the wing and the flap, letting high-pressure air from below flow over the flap's upper surface. That keeps the airflow attached and gives more lift. This is the most common type on light airplanes.
Fowler flap
Slides back on tracks before tilting down, so it increases wing area as well as camber. It gives the largest gain in lift for the least drag at the early settings.

Flaps change the airplane's pitch trim. Depending on the design, the nose may balloon up or drop when flaps go down. Be ready to hold the attitude and retrim.

Respect the flap speed limits. The top of the white arc on the airspeed indicator is the maximum flap extended speed (VFE), and some POHs list lower limits for larger flap settings. Retracting flaps reduces lift, so the airplane sinks. On a go-around, add full power and establish the climb attitude first, then raise the flaps in steps as the POH directs, while you have a positive rate of climb.

Leading edge devices and spoilers

Fixed slots, movable slats, and leading edge flaps let the wing reach a higher angle of attack before it stalls. They're more common on high-performance and short-takeoff airplanes than on trainers. Spoilers are panels on top of the wing that pop up to kill lift and add drag. Some airplanes use them for roll control along with or instead of ailerons, and gliders use them to steepen the descent.

Trim

Trim relieves the pressure you'd otherwise have to hold on the controls. Every training airplane has elevator trim. Some also have rudder or aileron trim.

How a trim tab works

A trim tab is a small hinged surface on the trailing edge of a control surface. It moves the opposite way from the way you want the control surface to go. To hold the elevator up for a nose-up attitude, the trim tab moves down. Air pushing up on the lowered tab forces the elevator's trailing edge up and holds it there, so you don't have to.

Some airplanes trim with a stabilator's antiservo tab, an adjustable horizontal stabilizer, or a spring in the control system. Small ground-adjustable tabs, bent by a mechanic to fix an imbalance in cruise, aren't for pilots to adjust.

Using trim well

Trim holds the elevator at a position, and in a stable airplane that corresponds to an airspeed. Once trimmed for a speed, the airplane tends to return to it. That's why trim is so useful on approach.

  1. Set the attitude

    Put the nose where it belongs for the airspeed you want, using the control.

  2. Set the power

    Adjust the throttle for the climb, cruise, or descent you want.

  3. Let it settle

    Hold the attitude while the airspeed stabilizes.

  4. Trim off the pressure

    Roll the trim until you can relax your hand without the nose moving. Then check: if the nose rises or falls when you let go, trim a little more.

Fly the attitude with the control and use trim only after it's set. Chasing the attitude with trim leads to constant corrections. Set the trim for takeoff before departure, using the POH setting or the takeoff mark on the indicator. A trim wheel left full nose-up can give you strong nose-up forces right after liftoff.

Controls on the ground

Preflight checks

Before you taxi, check that each control moves freely through its full travel and in the correct direction. Look at the surfaces while you move the controls, since a misrigged cable or a forgotten control lock is something you'll want to find on the ramp. Remove the control lock and any gust locks on the surfaces before you start the engine. During the walkaround, check the hinges, rod ends, cotter pins, and trim tab for damage, play, or missing hardware.

Taxiing in wind

Wind on the ground can lift a wing or the tail, especially in a high-wing airplane. You position the controls to keep the airplane planted. The usual rule for a tricycle-gear airplane:

Control positions while taxiing, tricycle gear
WindAileronsElevator
Quartering headwindTurn the yoke into the wind (upwind aileron up)Neutral
Quartering tailwindTurn the yoke away from the wind (upwind aileron down)Forward (elevator down)

A common memory aid is "climb into a headwind, dive away from a tailwind." With a headwind, the raised upwind aileron keeps the wind from lifting that wing. With a tailwind, the lowered upwind aileron and down elevator let the wind push those surfaces down, holding that wing and the tail on the ground. Tailwheel airplanes hold the elevator up (yoke back) with a headwind, so check how your instructor wants it done in your airplane.

With a quartering tailwind, the wind is hitting the control surfaces from behind. A strong gust can slam them against their stops, so hold the controls firmly and taxi slowly.

During the takeoff roll in a crosswind, start with full aileron into the wind and reduce it as speed builds and the ailerons get more effective. Use the rudder to keep the airplane on the centerline.
