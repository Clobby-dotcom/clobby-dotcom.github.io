Flight instruments

The six basic instruments tell you your speed, altitude, climb rate, attitude, heading, and rate of turn. Knowing how each one works lets you spot the moment one of them starts lying to you.

The pitot-static system

Three instruments run on air pressure: the altimeter, the vertical speed indicator (VSI), and the airspeed indicator. They get that air from two sources.

Pitot tube
A tube facing into the relative wind, usually under a wing. It collects ram (impact) pressure, which rises as the airplane moves faster through the air. Most pitot tubes have a small drain hole so water can escape. Only the airspeed indicator uses pitot pressure.
Static port
A flush hole, often on the side of the fuselage, placed where the air is undisturbed. It senses the still-air (static) pressure around the airplane. All three pitot-static instruments use it.
Alternate static source
A valve, usually under the panel, that opens the static line to air inside the cabin. You use it if the outside static port gets blocked.
Pitot heat
An electric heater in the pitot tube that keeps ice from closing it off. If you fly into visible moisture near freezing, turn it on before ice forms.

On preflight, check that the pitot cover is off, the pitot opening and drain hole are clear, and the static ports aren't plugged by tape, wax, or insects. Insects love to build nests in an uncovered pitot tube, and you usually find out when the airspeed stays at zero on the takeoff roll.

Altimeter

The altimeter is a barometer. Inside it is a stack of sealed aneroid wafers that expand as outside pressure drops and contract as it rises. Gears turn that movement into needle position, so the instrument shows altitude above whatever pressure level you set in the Kollsman window.

Setting it

Below 18,000 feet MSL, set the current reported altimeter setting of a station along your route within 100 nautical miles. At or above 18,000 feet MSL, set 29.92 inches of mercury (14 CFR 91.121). Before takeoff, set the local altimeter setting and compare the reading with the field elevation. The PHAK says that if the two differ by more than 75 feet, the altimeter's accuracy is questionable and it should be checked by a repair station.

Types of altitude

Indicated altitude
What the altimeter reads with the current local setting dialed in.
True altitude
Your actual height above mean sea level. Charts show terrain, obstacle, and airport heights as true altitude.
Absolute altitude
Your height above the ground directly below you (AGL).
Pressure altitude
What the altimeter reads with 29.92 set. It's the height above the standard datum plane and the starting point for performance calculations.
Density altitude
Pressure altitude corrected for nonstandard temperature. It's the altitude the airplane "feels" and drives engine, propeller, and wing performance. The Performance lesson covers it in depth.

Pressure and temperature errors

If you fly from high pressure toward low pressure without resetting the altimeter, it reads higher than your true altitude, so you're lower than you think. Colder-than-standard air does the same thing: the pressure levels squeeze closer together and the altimeter over-reads. The phrase to remember is "high to low, look out below," and it applies to both pressure and temperature. Going from low to high, the error runs the other way and you're higher than indicated.

Each 1 inch of mercury you're off on the altimeter setting is about 1,000 feet of altitude error. Updating the setting from ATC or ATIS as you fly is how you keep that error small.

Airspeed indicator and VSI

Airspeed indicator

The airspeed indicator compares pitot pressure with static pressure. Pitot pressure goes into a diaphragm and static pressure fills the case around it. The difference between them is dynamic pressure, and the needle reads it as airspeed.

Indicated airspeed (IAS)
What the instrument shows. POH speeds for takeoff, landing, and stall are given in IAS or CAS, so the airplane's handling stays tied to the needle at any altitude.
Calibrated airspeed (CAS)
IAS corrected for installation and instrument error. The POH has a table. Errors are largest at low speeds and high angles of attack.
True airspeed (TAS)
Your actual speed through the air, which is CAS corrected for altitude and temperature. As you climb and the air thins, TAS gets higher than IAS.
Groundspeed (GS)
TAS corrected for wind. It's what you use for time and fuel planning.

Maneuvering speed (VA) isn't marked on the indicator. It decreases as weight decreases, so look it up in the POH for your weight.

Vertical speed indicator

The VSI shows rate of climb or descent in feet per minute. Static pressure feeds a diaphragm directly and also leaks into the case through a small calibrated restriction. When you climb or descend, the case pressure lags behind the diaphragm pressure, and that difference moves the needle. Because of the lag, the VSI shows the trend right away but takes several seconds to settle on an accurate rate. Use it for trend, and don't chase the needle.

Pitot-static failures

A blocked line traps pressure, and the instrument reads that trapped pressure as if nothing changed. Work out each case by asking which pressure is stuck.

Pitot tube blocked, drain hole openDrops toward zero as the trapped air drains outNormalNormal
Pitot tube and drain hole both blockedActs like an altimeter: reads higher in a climb, lower in a descent, and doesn't change with real speed changes in level flightNormalNormal

The double pitot blockage is the dangerous one. In a climb the airspeed rises, and a pilot who believes it may pitch up even more and stall. With a static blockage in a descent, the airspeed reads high, which can lead you to slow down too much.

Fixing a static blockage

Open the alternate static source. Cabin air pressure is usually a little lower than outside pressure because of the airflow around the fuselage, so expect the altimeter and airspeed to read slightly high and the VSI to show a brief climb when you switch. The POH may have a correction table for alternate static. If there is no alternate source, breaking the VSI's glass lets cabin air into the static system. That makes the VSI unusable, and it's a last resort.

Gyroscopic instruments

The attitude indicator, heading indicator, and turn coordinator each use a spinning gyro. Two properties make them work.

Rigidity in space
A spinning gyro keeps pointing the same direction in space no matter how its mount moves. The attitude and heading indicators rely on this.
Precession
A force applied to a spinning gyro takes effect 90 degrees later in the direction of rotation. The turn coordinator uses precession to sense turns. Friction and wear also cause unwanted precession, which is why the heading indicator drifts.

Power sources

In many older trainers, an engine-driven vacuum pump spins the attitude and heading indicators, and the turn coordinator is electric. That split is deliberate: if the vacuum pump fails, you still have a working gyro. Check the suction gauge during runup and in flight, and know the normal range from the POH. A low reading means the vacuum gyros may slowly spin down and give wrong indications without any warning flag.

Attitude indicator

The gyro spins in a horizontal plane and holds the artificial horizon level while the airplane moves around it. It shows pitch and bank directly. After engine start, give it time to spin up and erect before you trust it. Older vacuum units can tumble past their pitch and bank limits and show small errors after acceleration and turns.

Heading indicator

The heading indicator has no magnetic sensing. It holds whatever heading you set, and precession makes it drift. Align it with the magnetic compass before flight and check it about every 15 minutes in flight, in straight and level, unaccelerated flight when the compass is steady. It doesn't have the compass's turning and acceleration errors, which is why you steer by it.

Turn coordinator and inclinometer

The turn coordinator's gyro is canted so it senses both roll and yaw. The little airplane shows rate of turn. Lining the wing up with the standard-rate mark gives a turn of 3 degrees per second, a full 360 in 2 minutes. It doesn't show bank angle or pitch. An older turn-and-slip indicator shows rate of turn only, with a needle.

The ball below it is the inclinometer. It shows whether the turn is coordinated. If the ball is off center, step on the rudder on the ball's side. A ball toward the inside of the turn means a slip, and toward the outside means a skid.

Glass cockpits

In a glass panel, an air data computer (ADC) handles the pitot-static data and an attitude and heading reference system (AHRS) replaces the gyros, usually with solid-state sensors and a magnetometer for heading. The failure logic is the same: if a sensor fails, the display usually shows a red X over that data. Learn your airplane's backup instruments and what each failure looks like on that system.

Magnetic compass

The compass is a magnet on a float in a fluid-filled case, and it needs no power. It's the reference you set the heading indicator to, and it's your heading source if the gyros fail. It also has more errors than any other instrument on the panel.

Variation
The angle between true north and magnetic north at your location. It's printed on sectional charts as isogonic lines. You correct for it when converting a true course to a magnetic course.
Deviation
Error caused by the airplane's own magnetic fields from metal and electrical equipment. It's different on each heading, and the compass correction card next to the compass lists it. Deviation can change with radios and other electrical loads on or off.
Magnetic dip
Earth's magnetic field lines tilt downward toward the poles, and the compass magnet tries to follow them. Dip is the cause of the turning and acceleration errors below, and it gets worse at higher latitudes.
Oscillation
The card swings around in turbulence or with rough control inputs, so read the average.

Turning errors (Northern Hemisphere)

When you start a turn from a northerly heading, the compass briefly shows a turn the opposite way, then lags behind. When you turn from a southerly heading, it shows the turn early and leads. On east and west headings, there's no turning error. The memory aid is UNOS: undershoot north, overshoot south. Rolling out on north, stop the turn before the compass reaches north. Rolling out on south, let it go past south before you roll out.

Acceleration errors

On an east or west heading, accelerating makes the compass show a turn toward north, and decelerating shows a turn toward south. The memory aid is ANDS: accelerate north, decelerate south. The error goes away once speed is steady. On north and south headings, acceleration error doesn't appear.

Trust the compass only in straight, level, unaccelerated flight. That's also the only time to reset the heading indicator from it.
