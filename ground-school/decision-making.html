Aeronautical decision-making

Many accidents trace back to a decision made well before anything went wrong: to take off into marginal weather, to keep going, or to wait too long to turn around. Aeronautical decision-making (ADM) gives you a structured way to make those calls before they make themselves.

What ADM is

The PHAK defines ADM as a systematic approach to the mental process pilots use to consistently determine the best course of action in response to a given set of circumstances. In plain terms, it's how you think through what's happening, what could go wrong, and what to do about it.

FAA studies of accident data have long shown that the large majority of general aviation accidents involve human factors, and that pilots who receive ADM training make fewer in-flight errors. That's why the Private Pilot ACS includes risk management elements in nearly every task, and why your examiner will ask scenario questions on the practical test, such as "What would you do if...?" There is often no single right answer. The examiner wants to see that you can identify the hazards, weigh the risk, and pick a safe course.

Two kinds of decisions show up in flying:

  • Analytical decisions, made with time to gather information and compare options. Preflight go/no-go decisions and most diversions fall here.
  • Automatic or naturalistic decisions, made quickly from training and experience. An engine failure on takeoff leaves no time to deliberate. The way to make those decisions good is to rehearse them on the ground, so the right response is already loaded when you need it.

Good ADM also starts before the flight. Set personal minimums: your own limits for ceiling, visibility, wind, crosswind, and fuel reserve, set more conservatively than the regulations while you're building experience. Write them down when you're calm on the ground. On the day of a flight, when you want to go, you compare the conditions to the list instead of arguing with yourself.

Hazardous attitudes

The PHAK identifies five hazardous attitudes that lead to poor judgment. Everyone has some of each. The skill is recognizing one in your own thinking and replacing it with its antidote, a short phrase you say to yourself. The knowledge test asks you to match attitudes to antidotes and to recognize an attitude from a description of a pilot's behavior.

The five hazardous attitudes and their antidotes
AttitudeWhat it sounds likeAntidote
Anti-authority"Don't tell me." Rules and procedures are for other people.Follow the rules. They are usually right.
Impulsivity"Do it quickly." Acting on the first idea without thinking it through.Not so fast. Think first.
Invulnerability"It won't happen to me." Accidents happen to other pilots.It could happen to me.
Macho"I can do it." Taking risks to prove you're good or to impress others.Taking chances is foolish.
Resignation"What's the use?" Feeling that nothing you do will change the outcome, or going along with others.I'm not helpless. I can make a difference.

A few examples of how these show up in practice. Skipping part of the checklist because the instructor isn't watching is anti-authority. Taking off into weather you haven't checked because your friends are already in the airplane can be macho or invulnerability. Letting a passenger talk you into a low pass is often resignation. Turning toward the first field you see after an engine hiccup, without checking for better options, is impulsivity.

Assessing risk

A hazard is a condition or event that could lead to harm, such as a low ceiling, a tired pilot, or an inoperative instrument. Risk is how likely that hazard is to cause harm and how bad the harm would be. Risk management is the process of finding the hazards and deciding what to do about each one.

PAVE: finding the hazards

The PAVE checklist divides the hazards of a flight into four groups, so you don't miss a whole category:

P, pilot in command
Your health, rest, recency, and experience with this airplane, this airport, and these conditions. Use IMSAFE here: illness, medication, stress, alcohol, fatigue, and emotion.
A, aircraft
Is it airworthy, with the required inspections and equipment? Is it suitable for the trip, with the performance, fuel range, and equipment you need? Are you familiar with it?
V, environment
Weather, terrain, airports and runways, airspace, and whether it's day or night.
E, external pressures
Anything pulling you to complete the flight: a meeting, passengers who need to get somewhere, a rental return time, or the wish to show off. External pressures are dangerous because they push you to accept risks you'd otherwise refuse.

One defense against external pressure is a backup plan made in advance. If you've already arranged a rental car, a hotel, or a way to tell people you'll be late, canceling or diverting costs you much less.

The risk matrix

The PHAK uses a simple matrix to rate each hazard. Likelihood runs from probable through occasional and remote to improbable. Severity runs from catastrophic through critical and marginal to negligible. A hazard that is probable and catastrophic is high risk. One that is improbable and negligible is low risk. The point isn't precise scoring. It's to make you ask both questions about each hazard: how likely, and how bad?

What to do with a risk

Once you've identified a risk, you have four options, sometimes remembered as TEAM:

  • Transfer it, for example by having a more experienced pilot fly that leg.
  • Eliminate it, for example by canceling or waiting for the weather to improve.
  • Accept it, if it's low enough after you've thought it through.
  • Mitigate it, by reducing the likelihood or the severity, such as taking extra fuel or picking a route with more airports along it.

Decision models

The FAA teaches several structured models. They overlap, and you don't need to run every one on every flight. Know what each letter stands for, because the knowledge test asks.

3P: perceive, process, perform

This is the PHAK's main model for managing risk in flight, and it's meant to repeat continuously:

  • Perceive the hazards. PAVE is the checklist for this step.
  • Process the information to decide whether each hazard creates a risk. The CARE checklist helps: think about the consequences, the alternatives, the reality of the situation, and the external pressures.
  • Perform: act to reduce the risk, using the TEAM options above, and then evaluate how it worked.

Then start over, because the situation keeps changing.

DECIDE

DECIDE is a six-step model, often used when something changes during the flight:

  1. Detect that a change has occurred.
  2. Estimate the need to counter or react to the change.
  3. Choose a desirable outcome for the flight.
  4. Identify actions that could successfully control the change.
  5. Do the necessary action.
  6. Evaluate the effect of the action.

Say you're en route and notice the clouds ahead are lower than forecast (detect). If you continue, you'll be squeezed between the clouds and the ground (estimate). You want to land safely and stay VFR (choose). You could turn back, divert to an airport off to the side, or land at the airport just behind you (identify). You turn toward the diversion airport and tell ATC or Flight Service (do). Then you check that the weather there is good and that you have the fuel (evaluate).

The 5P check

The 5P model reviews five variables at key points in a flight: the plan, the plane, the pilot, the passengers, and the programming (the avionics and automation). The PHAK suggests running it before the flight, before takeoff, at the midpoint, before descent, and before the final approach fix or, for VFR, before entering the traffic pattern. The value is in the timing: it makes you stop and reconsider at moments when it's easy to change your plan.

Operational pitfalls

The PHAK lists a set of behavior traps that experienced pilots fall into as well as new ones. Most of them come from the same wish: to finish the flight as planned.

  • Peer pressure leads to poor decisions based on emotion or on what others think, instead of the facts.
  • Mindset is the inability to recognize and cope with changes in a situation that differs from what you expected.
  • Get-there-itis clouds judgment with a fixation on the original goal or destination.
  • Duck-under syndrome is the urge to sneak a peek below minimums during an approach.
  • Scud running means pushing your capabilities and the airplane's to the limit by trying to keep visual contact with the ground while staying clear of clouds.
  • Continuing VFR into instrument conditions often leads to spatial disorientation or a collision with terrain or obstacles.
  • Getting behind the airplane means letting events control your actions instead of staying ahead of them.
  • Loss of positional or situational awareness is the extreme form of getting behind the airplane.
  • Operating without adequate fuel reserves, and ignoring the minimum reserves, leads to fuel exhaustion.
  • Descending below the minimum en route altitude raises the risk of hitting terrain or obstacles.
  • Flying outside the envelope assumes the airplane will perform beyond its limits.
  • Neglecting flight planning, preflight inspections, or checklists means relying on short-term memory, habit, and good luck.

A useful habit is to set decision points before you go. "If the ceiling at the halfway airport is below my minimums, I land there." "If I'm not at my checkpoint with the fuel I planned, I divert." A decision made in advance, on the ground, is much harder for get-there-itis to override.

Turning around or diverting early keeps your options open. If you wait until you're forced to turn, the weather behind you may have closed in too, and you'll have less fuel and daylight to work with.

Single-pilot resource management

Single-pilot resource management (SRM) is the art of managing all the resources available to a pilot alone in the cockpit, on board and outside, to make the flight safe. As a private pilot you'll usually be the only pilot on board, so you have to be your own crew.

Resources you have

  • Inside the airplane: the POH, checklists, charts, avionics and automation, and your passengers, who can watch for traffic, hold the chart, or read checklist items aloud if you brief them.
  • Outside the airplane: ATC, Flight Service, other pilots, and weather information in the cockpit. Asking ATC for help early, before a problem grows, is good resource management.

Situational awareness

Situational awareness is an accurate understanding of everything that affects the flight right now and in the near future: where you are, where the weather and traffic are, how the airplane and its systems are doing, and how much fuel and daylight you have left. Fatigue, stress, and workload all erode it. Complacency does too, especially on a familiar route.

Task management

Workload rises sharply in the busy phases, such as departure and arrival. Do everything you can ahead of time: brief the approach and pattern entry, set up radio frequencies, and run checklists before you get close to the airport. When workload spikes, prioritize. The standard order is aviate, navigate, communicate: first fly the airplane, then know where you're going, then talk to people.

Automation

A GPS or autopilot can lower your workload and free up attention, but only if you know how it works and keep monitoring it. Programming a box during a busy phase can be a distraction that causes more trouble than it solves. Know how to fly and navigate without it, and be ready to turn it off and hand-fly if it's not doing what you expect.
