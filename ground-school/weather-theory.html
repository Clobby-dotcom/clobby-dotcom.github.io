Weather theory

Weather causes a large share of serious accidents in small airplanes, and most of them start with a pilot who didn't see it coming. Knowing why weather happens helps you read a forecast and recognize when the sky is turning against you.

The atmosphere and pressure

The air is about 78 percent nitrogen and 21 percent oxygen, with the remaining 1 percent made up of other gases, including water vapor. Nearly all of the weather you'll fly in happens in the lowest layer, the troposphere.

The standard atmosphere

To compare performance and calibrate instruments, the FAA uses a standard atmosphere. At sea level it has a temperature of 15°C and a pressure of 29.92 inches of mercury (1013.2 millibars). In the lower atmosphere, temperature drops about 2°C and pressure drops about 1 inch of mercury for every 1,000 feet you climb. Real days rarely match the standard, which is why you get a local altimeter setting and why the airplane's performance changes from day to day.

Highs, lows, and wind

The sun heats the earth unevenly, so pressure varies from place to place. Air moves from high pressure toward low pressure, and the closer together the isobars on a weather map are, the stronger the wind. The earth's rotation bends that flow (the Coriolis force), so in the Northern Hemisphere:

  • Air flows clockwise and outward around a high. Air sinks in a high, which tends to bring clear skies, though it can also trap haze and smoke near the ground.
  • Air flows counterclockwise and inward around a low. Air rises in a low, which tends to bring clouds, precipitation, and poor weather.

Near the ground, friction slows the wind and weakens the Coriolis effect, so surface winds cross the isobars at an angle. That's one reason the wind often shifts direction and picks up speed as you climb.

Local effects matter too. On a coast, the land heats faster than the water during the day and a sea breeze blows onshore. At night it reverses. In the mountains, air flows up the slopes by day and down them at night. Strong wind over a ridge can make a mountain wave with powerful downdrafts on the lee side.

Pressure changes also affect your altimeter. If you fly from high pressure into low pressure without resetting it, the altimeter reads higher than your true altitude. "High to low, look out below" is the memory aid. The same thing happens flying from warm air into cold air.

Moisture and stability

Temperature and dewpoint

Air can only hold so much water vapor, and how much depends on its temperature. The dewpoint is the temperature the air would have to cool to before it becomes saturated. The difference between the temperature and the dewpoint is the spread. When the spread is small, usually within a few degrees, clouds, fog, or low visibility are likely, especially if the air is cooling, as it does in the evening.

Air cools as it rises. Unsaturated air cools at about 3°C per 1,000 feet. The dewpoint drops more slowly, so the spread closes by about 2.5°C for every 1,000 feet of lift. That gives a quick estimate of the base of cumulus clouds:

Cloud base (ft AGL) ≈ (temperature − dewpoint) ÷ 2.5 × 1,000

With a surface temperature of 25°C and a dewpoint of 15°C, the spread is 10, so the cloud bases should be about 4,000 feet above the ground.

Stable and unstable air

Stability is how the air responds to being lifted. In stable air, a lifted parcel ends up cooler than the air around it and sinks back down. In unstable air, it ends up warmer and keeps rising on its own. Air becomes less stable when it's warmed or moistened from below, and more stable when it's cooled from below.

A temperature inversion is a layer where temperature increases with altitude. It's very stable. On a calm, clear night the ground cools the air next to it and a surface inversion forms. Below it you'll often find haze, smoke, or fog and poor visibility. Above it the air may be smooth and clear. Expect a wind change, and possibly wind shear, as you climb or descend through it.

Air masses and fronts

An air mass is a large body of air that has taken on the temperature and moisture of the area where it formed. Air from over cold land is cold and dry, and air from over warm ocean is warm and moist. As it moves, it changes the weather in the places it passes over and is changed by them. Cold air moving over warm ground becomes unstable. Warm air moving over cold ground becomes stable.

A front is the boundary between two air masses. Crossing one, you'll see some combination of a temperature change, a wind shift, a pressure change, and a change in the dewpoint. The wind shift is often the most reliable sign.

Cold front
Cold, dense air pushes under warmer air and forces it up steeply. The weather comes in a fairly narrow band and can be violent if the warm air is moist and unstable: cumulus and cumulonimbus clouds, showers, thunderstorms, and gusty winds. A fast-moving cold front can have a squall line ahead of it. Behind the front, the weather usually clears quickly, with cooler temperatures, good visibility, and gusty winds.
Warm front
Warm air slides up over cooler air on a gentle slope. The weather spreads over a wide area, often hundreds of miles ahead of the surface front. High clouds come first, then the clouds lower and thicken into a steady rain or drizzle, low ceilings, and fog. If the warm air is unstable, thunderstorms can be hidden in the layers of cloud. Warm fronts move more slowly than cold fronts, so the poor weather lasts longer.
Stationary front
Neither air mass is replacing the other. The weather is often like a mild warm front and can stay over the same area for days.
Occluded front
A faster cold front catches up with a warm front and lifts the warm air off the ground. It can combine the weather of both, including embedded thunderstorms.

Clouds and fog

Cloud types

Clouds form when air is cooled to its dewpoint, most often by lifting. They're sorted by the height of their bases:

  • Low clouds, from near the surface up to about 6,500 feet AGL, include stratus, stratocumulus, and nimbostratus. They bring low ceilings and are where you're most likely to get trapped.
  • Middle clouds, from about 6,500 to 20,000 feet AGL, carry the prefix alto, as in altostratus and altocumulus.
  • High clouds, above about 20,000 feet, carry the prefix cirro or are called cirrus. They're made of ice crystals.
  • Clouds with extensive vertical development, such as towering cumulus and cumulonimbus, can span all three levels.

The name also tells you what the cloud is doing. Cumulus means heaped up, stratus means layered, and nimbus means it's producing precipitation. A cloud with "nimbus" in its name is raining or snowing.

Fog

Fog is a cloud at or near the ground. It forms when the air near the surface cools to its dewpoint or gets enough moisture added to saturate it. It can form quickly, which makes it dangerous for a VFR pilot planning to land in the evening or early morning.

Radiation fog
Forms on clear, calm nights when the ground cools by radiating its heat away. It's usually shallow, sits in low spots, and burns off as the sun warms the ground.
Advection fog
Forms when moist air moves over a colder surface, most often along coasts. Unlike radiation fog, it can form with wind and can cover large areas and last a long time.
Upslope fog
Forms when moist, stable air is pushed up a slope by the wind and cools as it rises. It's common on the eastern side of mountain ranges.
Precipitation-induced fog
Forms when warm rain falls through cooler air and the evaporation saturates it. It's often found with warm fronts and can cover a wide area.
Steam fog
Forms when cold air moves over warmer water. It's usually shallow, and low-level turbulence and icing can come with it.

Icing

Structural icing needs two things: visible moisture, such as cloud or rain, and an airplane surface at or below freezing. Ice changes the shape of the wing, which reduces lift and raises the stall speed. It also adds weight and drag, and it can block the pitot tube or static port and freeze antennas and control surfaces. Most training airplanes aren't certified for flight in icing conditions, and a VFR pilot should never be in clouds anyway.

  • Rime ice is rough, milky, and brittle. It forms when small droplets freeze on impact.
  • Clear ice is smooth, glossy, and heavy. It forms when large droplets flow back before they freeze, and it's harder to remove.
  • Mixed ice is a combination of the two.

Freezing rain is especially dangerous because ice accumulates very quickly. It means there's a layer of warmer air above you where the rain formed. Treat any forecast or report of freezing rain or freezing drizzle along your route as a reason not to go.

Frost on the ground matters too. Even a thin layer of frost on the wings roughens the surface enough to disrupt airflow and can keep the airplane from flying at normal takeoff speed. Remove all frost before flight.

Carburetor icing is a separate problem. It can happen in clear air well above freezing when the humidity is high. It's covered with the engine systems.

Thunderstorms and wind shear

A thunderstorm needs three things: enough water vapor, an unstable lapse rate, and something to start the air rising, such as heating of the ground, a front, or terrain. Every thunderstorm goes through three stages:

  1. Cumulus stage: updrafts build the cloud upward.
  2. Mature stage: begins when precipitation reaches the surface. Strong updrafts and downdrafts exist side by side. This is the most violent stage, with lightning, heavy rain, possible hail, and severe turbulence.
  3. Dissipating stage: downdrafts take over and the storm rains itself out.

Thunderstorms can produce almost every weather hazard at once: severe turbulence, icing, hail, lightning, heavy rain, strong gusty surface winds, and tornadoes. Hail can fall from the anvil miles away from the storm's core, even into clear air. A squall line is a line of storms that often forms ahead of a cold front. It can be too long to go around and too high to go over.

Never try to fly under a thunderstorm or through a gap between storms. The FAA's guidance is to stay at least 20 miles away from any thunderstorm identified as severe or giving an intense radar echo.

Microbursts and wind shear

Wind shear is a sudden change in wind speed or direction over a short distance. It's most dangerous near the ground, where you're slow and have little altitude to recover. It's found near thunderstorms, fronts, temperature inversions, and gusty winds around buildings and terrain.

A microburst is a small, intense downdraft, usually from a thunderstorm, that spreads out in every direction when it hits the ground. Its downdrafts can reach 6,000 feet per minute. Flying through one on approach, you'd first get a headwind that makes the airplane climb and gain airspeed, then a strong downdraft, then a tailwind that takes the airspeed away. An airplane that started with too much energy can end up low and slow over the ground. The strongest winds in a microburst usually last only a few minutes, so waiting is a sound choice. If there are thunderstorms near the airport, hold off on the approach or the takeoff until they've passed.
