Aerodynamics

Aerodynamics explains why the airplane does what it does when you move the controls or change the power. Once you understand angle of attack and load factor, stalls, steep turns, and slow flight stop being mysterious.

The four forces

Four forces act on an airplane in flight. Lift acts upward, perpendicular to the relative wind. Weight pulls straight down toward the center of the earth. Thrust pulls the airplane forward. Drag holds it back, parallel to the relative wind.

In steady, unaccelerated flight, including a steady climb or descent, the opposing forces balance. When one force changes, the airplane accelerates until the forces settle into a new balance.

Airfoil terms

Chord line
An imaginary straight line from the leading edge to the trailing edge of the wing.
Camber
The curve of the airfoil's upper and lower surfaces. Most training airplane wings have more camber on top.
Relative wind
The airflow the wing meets, parallel to and opposite the flight path. It's set by the flight path, so it can point a different way than the nose.
Angle of attack
The angle between the chord line and the relative wind. You control it directly with the elevator.
Angle of incidence
The fixed angle between the wing's chord line and the airplane's longitudinal axis. It's set when the airplane is built, and you can't change it.

How a wing makes lift

Two explanations work together. Air flowing over the curved upper surface speeds up, and by Bernoulli's principle its pressure drops, so the higher pressure below pushes the wing up. At the same time, the wing turns the airflow downward (downwash), and by Newton's third law the air pushes the wing up in return.

The lift equation shows what you can change:

Lift = CL × ½ρV² × S

CL is the coefficient of lift, which rises with angle of attack and with flaps. ρ is air density, V is true airspeed, and S is wing area. Speed is squared, so doubling airspeed gives four times the lift at the same angle of attack. That's why slow flight takes a high angle of attack and cruise a low one.

Drag, ground effect, and wake turbulence

Drag comes in two kinds, and they behave in opposite ways as speed changes.

Parasite drag
Drag from the airplane moving through the air: form drag from its shape, interference drag where parts meet (like the wing and fuselage), and skin friction from the surface. It increases with the square of airspeed.
Induced drag
The byproduct of making lift. High pressure under the wing spills around the tip toward the low pressure on top, which tilts the lift backward and creates wingtip vortices. Induced drag is greatest at slow speed and high angle of attack, and it decreases as you speed up.

Total drag is lowest at one speed, where the lift-to-drag ratio is highest (L/Dmax). The POH best glide speed is close to it. Slower than that, induced drag climbs fast and you need more power to fly slower. This is the region of reversed command, and you'll feel it in slow flight.

Ground effect

Within about one wingspan of the ground, the surface interferes with the wingtip vortices and the downwash. Induced drag drops and the wing makes more lift for a given angle of attack. On landing, an airplane that comes in too fast floats down the runway. On takeoff, it can lift off before it has enough speed to climb, then settle back when it leaves ground effect. Soft-field takeoffs use this on purpose: you lift off early and accelerate in ground effect.

Wake turbulence

Wingtip vortices are strongest behind an airplane that is heavy, clean, and slow, like a large airplane on takeoff or landing. They sink slowly and drift with the wind, and a light crosswind can hold one over the runway. The AIM's avoidance guidance comes down to staying above the larger airplane's flight path:

  • Landing behind a larger airplane on the same runway, stay at or above its approach path and touch down beyond the point where it touched down.
  • Taking off behind a larger airplane that just departed, lift off before its rotation point and climb above its path, or turn away from it.
  • Landing behind a larger airplane that just took off, touch down well before its rotation point.

Angle of attack, stalls, and spins

Every wing has a critical angle of attack. Below it, more angle of attack means more lift. Past it, the air can no longer follow the upper surface. The flow separates, lift drops sharply, and drag rises. That's a stall.

A wing stalls when it exceeds its critical angle of attack. That can happen at any airspeed, in any attitude, at any power setting. The way out is always to reduce the angle of attack.

Published stall speeds assume a stated weight, CG, power setting, and wings level. Stall speed goes up with more weight, more load factor, a forward CG, and ice or frost on the wing. Even thin frost roughens the wing enough to matter, so it all comes off before flight.

Recognizing a stall

Warning signs include mushy controls, a nose-high attitude with decreasing airspeed, the stall warning horn or light, buffeting, and a quieter airflow sound. Training airplane wings are built so the root stalls before the tip, using washout (a twist that gives the tip less angle of incidence) or stall strips. That keeps the ailerons working longer.

Recovering

Reduce the angle of attack first, by moving the elevator control forward enough to break the stall. Then level the wings with coordinated aileron and rudder, add power as needed, and return to the desired flight path without stalling again. Adding power without lowering the angle of attack doesn't fix a stall.

Spins

A spin is an aggravated stall with autorotation, and it needs a stall plus yaw. The more deeply stalled wing makes less lift and more drag, so the airplane rolls and yaws toward it and keeps rotating. The classic setup is a skidding turn at low altitude, such as adding rudder to tighten an overshot turn from base to final. Keep the ball centered.

Follow the POH spin recovery procedure. Many POHs use the steps taught with the PARE mnemonic:

  • P: power to idle
  • A: ailerons neutral
  • R: rudder full opposite the direction of rotation
  • E: elevator briskly forward to break the stall

Once the rotation stops, neutralize the rudder and ease out of the dive. Many airplanes aren't approved for intentional spins, so check the POH and placards.

Turns and load factor

Banking tilts the lift so part of it pulls the airplane sideways, and that turns it. Only the vertical part is left to hold up the weight, so to stay level you add back pressure to raise the angle of attack, and sometimes add power.

Load factor is the total lift divided by the weight, measured in Gs. In a level turn, it depends only on bank angle, not on the airplane or its speed.

Load factor and stall speed in a level, coordinated turn
Bank angleLoad factorStall speed increase
0°1.0 Gnone
30°1.15 Gabout 7%
45°1.41 Gabout 19%
60°2.0 Gabout 41%

Stall speed rises with the square root of the load factor. The curve gets steep past 45 degrees, which is why a steep turn at low speed near the ground is dangerous.

Load limits and maneuvering speed

Each airplane has a limit load factor. The PHAK lists +3.8 G and −1.52 G for the normal category, +4.4 G and −1.76 G for utility, and +6.0 G and −3.0 G for acrobatic. Some trainers hold more than one category, each with its own weight and CG limits, so check the POH.

Design maneuvering speed (VA) is the speed below which a single full, abrupt control deflection should stall the wing before it overloads the structure. It doesn't cover full deflections in more than one axis or back-and-forth inputs. VA goes down as weight goes down, so use the POH value for your weight, and slow to it or below in turbulence.

Overbanking tendency

In a turn, the outside wing travels faster than the inside wing and makes more lift. In a shallow bank, roughly under 20 degrees, stability rolls the airplane back toward level. In a medium bank it tends to stay put. In a steep bank, past about 45 degrees, the bank keeps increasing unless you hold some opposite aileron.

Stability

Stability describes what the airplane does after something disturbs it, like a gust.

Static stability
The airplane's first tendency after a disturbance. Positive static stability means it tends to return to where it was. Neutral means it stays in the new attitude. Negative means it keeps moving away.
Dynamic stability
What happens over time. An airplane with positive dynamic stability returns through oscillations that get smaller and die out. Training airplanes are designed with positive static stability and mostly positive dynamic stability.

Pitch (longitudinal) stability

In most airplanes, the CG sits ahead of the wing's center of lift, so the nose tends to drop, and the horizontal tail balances this with a downward force. If speed builds, the tail's downforce grows and raises the nose. If speed drops, the downforce shrinks and the nose falls. That's why a trimmed airplane tends to hold its airspeed.

A forward CG makes the airplane more stable and harder to flare. An aft CG makes it less stable, and past the aft limit, stall or spin recovery may not be possible.

Roll (lateral) stability

Dihedral is the upward angle of the wings from root to tip. When a gust drops one wing, the airplane starts to slip toward it, and the lower wing meets the relative wind at a higher angle of attack. It makes more lift and rolls the airplane back toward level. High-wing airplanes also get help from the keel effect: the fuselage hangs below the wing like a pendulum.

Yaw (directional) stability

The vertical fin works like a weathervane. If the nose yaws off the relative wind, air pushes on the side of the fin and swings the nose back.

Spiral instability

Directional stability is usually stronger than lateral stability. Left uncorrected, a dropped wing slowly steepens into a tightening, descending turn: the graveyard spiral that catches VFR pilots who fly into clouds. Pulling back only tightens it. Level the wings first, then raise the nose.

Left-turning tendencies

On most American-built single-engine airplanes, the propeller turns clockwise as seen from the cockpit. Four effects make the airplane want to yaw or roll left, mostly at high power and low airspeed, like on takeoff and in a climb. You counter them with right rudder.

Torque reaction
Newton's third law: the engine turns the propeller clockwise, so the propeller tries to roll the airplane counterclockwise, to the left. On the takeoff roll this pushes the left tire down harder, adding friction on that side and a pull to the left.
Spiraling slipstream
The propeller's slipstream corkscrews around the fuselage and strikes the left side of the vertical fin, pushing the tail right and the nose left. The effect is strongest at high power and low airspeed.
P-factor
Asymmetric thrust. At a high angle of attack, the descending blade on the right side meets the air at a bigger angle than the ascending blade on the left, so it makes more thrust. The center of thrust shifts right and yaws the nose left. You'll notice it most in a climb.
Gyroscopic precession
The spinning propeller acts like a gyroscope. A force applied to it shows up 90 degrees later in the direction of rotation. When the nose pitches up or down, precession causes a yaw. It's most noticeable in a tailwheel airplane when the tail comes up on takeoff, which yaws the nose left.

Manufacturers offset some of this for cruise, so you need little rudder in cruise and plenty of right rudder on takeoff and in the climb. Keep the ball centered with your feet.
