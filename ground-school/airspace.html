Ground school

Airspace

Airspace classes decide what you need to enter an area: a clearance, a radio call, certain equipment, or a minimum amount of visibility and distance from clouds. Being able to read them off the sectional chart is a big part of the knowledge test and of every cross-country you plan.

How airspace is organized

Airspace is either controlled or uncontrolled. Controlled airspace (Classes A, B, C, D, and E) is where ATC provides service to IFR flights. A VFR pilot doesn't always talk to ATC there. In Class E, you can fly VFR without a word to anyone.

Class G is uncontrolled. ATC has no authority over traffic there, and the VFR weather minimums are lowest.

In general, the busier the airport, the more the airspace around it requires.

Most altitudes on a sectional chart are in feet above mean sea level (MSL). Two numbers stacked like a fraction show the ceiling over the floor, in hundreds of feet. "70 over 30" means the airspace goes from 3,000 to 7,000 feet MSL. "SFC" means it starts at the surface.

The classes, A through G

Class A

From 18,000 feet MSL up to and including flight level 600, over the 48 contiguous states and Alaska and over nearby waters. Everyone in Class A flies IFR. As a VFR private pilot, you won't go there. It isn't drawn on sectional charts.

Class B

Around the country's busiest airports. Each one is custom-built, but the usual shape is an upside-down wedding cake: a small inner core from the surface, with wider shelves stacked above it, often up to 10,000 feet MSL. It's drawn with solid blue lines, and each shelf is labeled with its ceiling and floor.

  • You need an ATC clearance to enter. Listen for the words "cleared into the Class Bravo airspace." Being told to squawk a code or being in radar contact isn't a clearance.
  • You need at least a private pilot certificate, or a student pilot certificate with the required training and endorsements (14 CFR 91.131, 61.95).
  • Some of the busiest Class B airports don't allow student pilots to take off or land at all. They're listed in 14 CFR Part 91, Appendix D, Section 4.

Class C

Around busy airports with a control tower and radar approach control. It's drawn with solid magenta lines. A typical Class C has two parts:

  • An inner core with a 5 nautical mile radius, from the surface up to 4,000 feet above the airport elevation.
  • An outer shelf out to 10 nautical miles, from 1,200 feet above the airport up to the same 4,000 foot ceiling.

Each one is tailored to its airport, so read the actual numbers on the chart. To enter, you must establish two-way radio communication with ATC before you cross the boundary (14 CFR 91.130). That means the controller answers with your call sign. If the controller says "Aircraft calling, stand by," you have not established communication, because your call sign wasn't used. If they say "N1234X, stand by," you have, and you may enter.

Class D

Around airports with an operating control tower. It's drawn with dashed blue lines. It usually reaches from the surface up to about 2,500 feet above the airport, shown on the chart in MSL inside a dashed box. A number with a minus sign in front, like [-25], means up to but not including 2,500 feet MSL.

You need two-way radio communication with the tower before entering (14 CFR 91.129). When the tower closes, the airspace usually becomes Class E or Class G. The Chart Supplement tells you which, and the tower's hours.

Class E

Class E is all the controlled airspace that isn't A, B, C, or D. It's the most common class and the hardest to see on a chart, because it comes in several forms:

  • Surface area Class E around some airports without a tower, shown by a dashed magenta line. It starts at the surface.
  • Class E starting at 700 feet above ground, shown by a faded magenta band. These areas usually surround airports with instrument approaches, to protect IFR traffic descending to the airport.
  • Class E starting at 1,200 feet above ground, which covers most of the country. Where it changes to a different floor, a faded blue band marks the edge.
  • Federal airways (Victor airways), which are Class E from 1,200 feet above ground unless charted otherwise.
  • Everywhere at and above 14,500 feet MSL, except within 1,500 feet of the surface.
  • Above flight level 600.

Unless it's charted lower, Class E goes up to, but not including, 18,000 feet MSL, where Class A begins.

Class G

Whatever is left over: uncontrolled airspace below the floor of Class E. In most places that means the surface up to 700 or 1,200 feet above ground. In remote areas it can reach up to 14,500 feet MSL.

When you look at a point on a sectional, work upward from the ground. Ask what class starts at the surface, where it ends, and what sits on top of it. Class G under a 700 foot Class E floor has different weather minimums at 600 feet than at 800 feet.

VFR weather minimums

These are the visibility and cloud clearances you need to fly VFR in each class (14 CFR 91.155). Cloud clearance is there so you have time to see and avoid an IFR airplane popping out of a cloud.

Most of the table reduces to "3 miles, and 500 below, 1,000 above, 2,000 horizontal," often remembered as 3-152. The exceptions are what show up on the test: Class B (clear of clouds), high altitude (5 miles and 1,000, 1,000, 1 mile), and low-level Class G by day (1 mile, clear of clouds).

More rules that go with the table

  • Within the surface area of Class B, C, D, or E airspace, you can't take off, land, or enter the traffic pattern unless the ceiling is at least 1,000 feet and the ground visibility is at least 3 statute miles (or flight visibility, if ground visibility isn't reported) (14 CFR 91.155(c) and (d)).
  • In Class G at night, when visibility is less than 3 but at least 1 statute mile, you may fly clear of clouds if you stay in the traffic pattern within half a mile of the runway (14 CFR 91.155(b)(2)).
  • Student pilots have their own limit: no solo flight with flight or surface visibility under 3 statute miles by day or 5 statute miles at night (14 CFR 61.89). Your instructor's endorsement may set higher limits.

Special VFR

Special VFR lets you operate in the surface area of controlled airspace when the weather is below basic VFR minimums. You need an ATC clearance, must stay clear of clouds, and need at least 1 statute mile flight visibility. To take off or land, ground visibility must be at least 1 statute mile, or flight visibility if ground visibility isn't reported (14 CFR 91.157). At night, special VFR requires an instrument rating and an airplane equipped for IFR. Special VFR is legal for a private pilot by day, but low visibility is where VFR pilots end up in cloud, so use it only with training and personal minimums well above the legal ones.

Equipment and speed rules

Transponder and ADS-B Out

An altitude-reporting (Mode C) transponder and ADS-B Out are required in these places (14 CFR 91.215, 91.225):

  • In Class A, B, and C airspace.
  • Within 30 nautical miles of a Class B primary airport, from the surface up to 10,000 feet MSL. This ring is the Mode C veil, drawn as a thin magenta circle on the sectional.
  • Above the ceiling and within the lateral limits of Class B or Class C, up to 10,000 feet MSL.
  • Above 10,000 feet MSL over the 48 contiguous states and DC, except at and below 2,500 feet above the surface.

Some airplanes certificated without an electrical system are exempt in parts of this airspace. For normal VFR flight, squawk 1200 unless ATC gives you a code.

Speed limits

From 14 CFR 91.117, unless ATC authorizes otherwise:

  • No more than 250 knots indicated below 10,000 feet MSL.
  • No more than 200 knots indicated at or below 2,500 feet above the surface within 4 nautical miles of the primary airport in Class C or Class D.
  • No more than 200 knots indicated in the airspace underneath a Class B shelf, or in a VFR corridor through Class B.

Special use and other airspace

Special use airspace is where activities happen that other aircraft need to stay out of or watch for. Most areas are drawn on sectionals with a letter and number, and the chart margin lists their altitudes, times of use, and controlling agency.

Prohibited area (P-)
Flight is not allowed, for security reasons. Examples include the airspace over the White House and Camp David.
Restricted area (R-)
Hazards such as artillery firing, missiles, or aerial gunnery. When a restricted area is active, you need permission from the controlling agency to enter. When it isn't active, ATC may let you through.
Warning area (W-)
Like a restricted area, but over international waters, beginning 3 nautical miles from the coast. It warns of hazards without prohibiting entry.
Military operations area (MOA)
Military training such as air combat practice. VFR flight is allowed, but be very watchful. Contact a nearby flight service station or the controlling agency to see if it's active.
Alert area (A-)
A high volume of pilot training or unusual activity. You can fly through, and everyone there shares responsibility for avoiding collisions.
Controlled firing area
Activity that would be hazardous to aircraft, such as blasting. It isn't charted, because the activity stops when a spotter sees an airplane approaching.
National security area
Over sensitive ground facilities. Pilots are asked to avoid flying through voluntarily. Flight can be temporarily prohibited by NOTAM.

Other airspace areas

  • Temporary flight restrictions (TFRs) come and go by NOTAM. They cover things like presidential movements, wildfires, and large sporting events (14 CFR 91.137 through 91.145). They can pop up on short notice, so check for them before every flight.
  • Military training routes are low, fast routes for military jets. Routes with a four-digit number are flown at or below 1,500 feet above ground, and those with three digits include segments above 1,500 feet. IR routes are flown under IFR and VR routes under VFR.
  • The Washington, DC, Special Flight Rules Area has its own procedures, and pilots flying VFR within 60 nautical miles of the DCA VOR/DME must complete special awareness training (14 CFR 91.161).
  • Air defense identification zones (ADIZ) surround the country's borders and coasts, and require a flight plan and specific procedures to cross.
  • Over national parks and wildlife refuges, pilots are asked to stay at least 2,000 feet above the surface.

Before any flight, look at the chart for your whole route and get a briefing that includes NOTAMs and TFRs. Then plan how you'll go around, over, under, or through each area you meet.
