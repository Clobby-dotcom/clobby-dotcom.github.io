Ground school

Documents, inspections, and equipment

Before every flight, you decide whether the airplane is legal and safe to fly. That means knowing which papers it carries, which inspections must be current, and what to do when something on the panel doesn't work.

Who decides airworthiness

Two rules set up the whole topic. The pilot in command is responsible for determining whether the aircraft is in condition for safe flight, and must end the flight when unairworthy mechanical, electrical, or structural conditions occur (14 CFR 91.7). The owner or operator is primarily responsible for keeping the aircraft in an airworthy condition, including compliance with airworthiness directives (14 CFR 91.403).

In practice, the owner or the flight school arranges the maintenance and keeps the records, and you check that the work was done. When you rent an airplane, you're the operator for that flight, so "the school handles that" isn't an answer an examiner will accept. Know how to find the dates in the logbooks or the tracking sheet your airplane uses.

An airplane is airworthy when it conforms to its type design, including any approved alterations, and is in condition for safe operation. Your preflight inspection checks the second part. The documents and maintenance records show the first.

Documents on board

The common memory aid is ARROW.

A: Airworthiness certificate
Issued when the airplane is certificated. It stays valid as long as the airplane is maintained and operated under the regulations. It must be displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance where passengers or crew can read it (14 CFR 91.203).
R: Registration certificate
Shows who owns the airplane. It must be on board (14 CFR 91.203). A registration certificate now expires seven years after the last day of the month it was issued (14 CFR 47.40). It also ends early if the airplane is sold, so check the name and the date.
R: Radio station license
An FCC license, needed only when you fly outside the United States, for example into Canada, Mexico, or the Bahamas. It isn't required for domestic flights.
O: Operating limitations
The approved flight manual or POH, plus the required placards and instrument markings (14 CFR 91.9). Together they tell you the airplane's speed, weight, and engine limits.
W: Weight and balance data
The current empty weight, empty-weight CG, and equipment list for that specific airplane. It's usually kept with the POH. See the Weight and balance lesson.

You also carry your own documents: your pilot certificate (for a student, the student pilot certificate), a medical certificate unless you're operating under BasicMed, and a government-issued photo ID (14 CFR 61.3). As a student, your logbook with the required solo endorsements must be with you on every solo flight.

The maintenance logbooks don't have to be in the airplane, and many owners keep them in the office. You still need to be able to show that the inspections are current.

Calendar months

A calendar-month interval runs to the end of the month. An annual completed on March 10 this year is good through March 31 next year.

Annual and 100-hour

Every airplane needs an annual. The annual must be done by a mechanic who holds an inspection authorization (IA). A 100-hour inspection can be done by a mechanic with airframe and powerplant ratings, and an annual also counts as a 100-hour inspection, but a 100-hour doesn't count as an annual.

The 100-hour limit may be exceeded by up to 10 hours if you're flying to a place where the inspection can be done. Those extra hours count toward the next 100 hours (14 CFR 91.409(b)). That's the only flexibility. A rental or school airplane used for instruction must stay within its 100 hours, so check the hours remaining before you fly.

ELT batteries

Besides the 12-month inspection, ELT batteries must be replaced (or recharged, if rechargeable) when the transmitter has been used for more than 1 cumulative hour or when 50 percent of their useful life has expired. The new expiration date is marked on the outside of the transmitter and entered in the maintenance records (14 CFR 91.207(c)). An ELT isn't required for training flights conducted entirely within 50 nautical miles of the airport where the local flight began (14 CFR 91.207(f)), but most trainers have one installed anyway, and an installed ELT should still be inspected and working.

91.205 is only one source of required equipment. The airplane's type certificate, its equipment list, its kinds of operations equipment list (often in POH Section 2 or 6), airworthiness directives, and other rules can each require more. A transponder, for example, isn't on either list above, but 14 CFR 91.215 requires one in much of the busier airspace.

Flying with inoperative equipment

Things break. A dome light burns out, a second radio goes quiet, the clock stops. Whether you can still fly depends on the rules in 14 CFR 91.213.

With a minimum equipment list

Some airplanes operate under an FAA-approved minimum equipment list (MEL), which comes with a letter of authorization. If yours has one, it's the governing document. It lists exactly what may be inoperative and under what conditions, and you follow it.

Without an MEL

Most small piston trainers don't have an MEL. They use 91.213(d). You may take off with an inoperative instrument or piece of equipment only if all of these are true:

  • It isn't part of the VFR-day type certification instruments and equipment for that airplane.
  • It isn't listed as required on the airplane's equipment list or kinds of operations equipment list for the kind of flight you're making.
  • It isn't required by 91.205 or any other rule for the kind of flight you're making.
  • It isn't required to be operational by an airworthiness directive.

If it passes those tests, it must be either removed, with the cockpit control placarded and the maintenance recorded, or deactivated and placarded "Inoperative." If deactivating it involves maintenance, the work must be done and recorded under Part 43. Then a certificated and appropriately rated pilot or mechanic must determine that the inoperative item isn't a hazard.

  1. Find out whether it's required

    Check the type certification requirements, the equipment list, 91.205 for day or night VFR, any other rule for the flight, and ADs.

  2. If it's required, don't go

    Get it fixed, or ask a mechanic about a special flight permit (14 CFR 21.197) to ferry the airplane to a repair shop.

  3. If it isn't required, remove or deactivate it

    Placard it "Inoperative." Anything that takes a tool or touches wiring is maintenance and needs a mechanic and a logbook entry.

  4. Decide whether it's a hazard

    Think about tonight's weather, the time of day, and where you're going. A dead landing light may be legal for a non-commercial night flight, but you still decide whether it's safe for this one.

An inoperative item found during preflight is your decision as PIC. Write it up the way your school or owner wants it reported, so the next pilot doesn't find it the hard way.

ADs, records, and preventive maintenance

Airworthiness directives

An airworthiness directive (AD) is a legally enforceable rule the FAA issues when it finds an unsafe condition in a type of aircraft, engine, propeller, or appliance (14 CFR Part 39). An AD says what must be done and by when. Some are one-time fixes. Others are recurring, due every so many hours or months, so they need tracking just like the annual. Emergency ADs take effect as soon as the owner receives them. Flying an airplane with an overdue AD makes it unairworthy, so look for the AD compliance record in the logbooks.

Maintenance records

Every maintenance action is entered in the aircraft records with a description of the work, the completion date, and the name, certificate number, kind of certificate, and signature of the person approving it for return to service (14 CFR 43.9). Most airplanes have separate airframe, engine, and propeller logbooks. When you look for the annual, you're looking for an entry stating that the airplane was inspected and found airworthy, signed by an IA.

Preventive maintenance

A pilot holding at least a private pilot certificate may perform preventive maintenance on an aircraft that pilot owns or operates, as long as the aircraft isn't used under Part 121, 129, or 135 (14 CFR 43.3(g)). Preventive maintenance is a specific list of simple tasks in Part 43, Appendix A, such as changing oil, replacing some bulbs, and servicing tires. The pilot must make a logbook entry for the work under 43.9. As a student pilot, you can't sign off preventive maintenance, and on a rental you'll follow the operator's rules even after you're certificated.

Squawks and the preflight

Before you fly a rental or school airplane, read the open squawk list or discrepancy log. Something a previous pilot reported may be inoperative and placarded, or it may still be waiting for a mechanic's decision. If you can't tell whether an open write-up grounds the airplane, ask before you start the engine.
