Performance

The same airplane that leaps off a cold runway at sea level can struggle to clear the trees on a hot afternoon at a high field. Performance planning tells you, before you commit, whether the runway is long enough and the climb is good enough for today's conditions.

Density altitude

Everything the airplane does depends on air density. Thinner air means the engine makes less power, the propeller makes less thrust, and the wing needs a higher true airspeed to make the same lift. Density altitude is the way pilots measure how thin the air is: it's the altitude in the standard atmosphere where the density would match what you have now.

The standard atmosphere

Performance charts and altimeters are built around the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA). At sea level, standard is 15°C and 29.92 inches of mercury. Temperature drops about 2°C per 1,000 feet, and pressure drops about 1 inch of mercury per 1,000 feet in the lower atmosphere.

Pressure altitude first

Pressure altitude is what the altimeter reads with 29.92 set. On the ground, you can set 29.92 and read it, or work it out:

Pressure altitude = field elevation + (29.92 − altimeter setting) × 1,000

With a field elevation of 1,200 feet and an altimeter setting of 29.72, pressure altitude is 1,200 + (0.20 × 1,000) = 1,400 feet. Lower pressure than standard raises pressure altitude, and higher pressure lowers it.

Then correct for temperature

Density altitude is pressure altitude corrected for nonstandard temperature. Use the E6B, an electronic flight computer, or the chart in the PHAK. A common rule of thumb adds about 120 feet of density altitude for each degree Celsius above standard temperature at your pressure altitude.

Density altitude ≈ pressure altitude + 120 × (OAT − ISA temperature)

Continuing the example: standard temperature at 1,400 feet is about 15 − 2.8 = 12°C. On a 32°C day that's 20°C above standard, so density altitude is roughly 1,400 + 2,400 = 3,800 feet. The airplane will perform as if it were taking off from a field almost 4,000 feet high.

What raises density altitude

  • Higher elevation, because the air pressure is lower.
  • Lower barometric pressure, from weather systems passing through.
  • Higher temperature, because warm air expands and gets less dense.
  • Higher humidity. Water vapor is lighter than dry air, so moist air is less dense. Most charts don't account for it, so expect a little less performance than the chart shows on humid days.

High density altitude hurts you three ways at once: less engine power, less propeller efficiency, and a higher true airspeed needed for liftoff. The takeoff roll gets longer and the climb gets flatter.

Takeoff

The POH gives two takeoff numbers. Ground roll is the distance to liftoff. Total distance to clear a 50-foot obstacle is the one to compare with the runway when anything is off the far end, which is almost always.

What changes takeoff distance

Density altitude
The airplane lifts off at the same indicated airspeed, but that's a higher true airspeed and groundspeed in thin air, and the engine has less power to get there.
Weight
A heavier airplane needs more speed to lift off and accelerates more slowly. The PHAK notes that a 10 percent increase in takeoff weight causes about a 5 percent increase in takeoff speed and at least a 21 percent increase in takeoff distance.
Wind
A headwind lowers the groundspeed you need at liftoff and shortens the roll. A tailwind lengthens it by more than the same headwind shortens it. The POH chart notes say how to correct for each.
Runway surface and condition
Grass, gravel, soft ground, snow, and standing water all add rolling resistance. Many POHs give a correction for dry grass. Wet or soft surfaces can add much more than that, and they're rarely in the chart.
Runway slope
An uphill runway adds distance and a downhill one shortens it. The Chart Supplement lists runway gradient where it's significant.
Flap setting and technique
The chart assumes the flap setting, power setting, and technique written on it. Different technique gives different numbers.

Ground effect

Within about one wingspan of the surface, the ground interrupts the wingtip vortices and downwash, so induced drag drops and the wing makes lift more easily. On a heavy or high-density-altitude takeoff, the airplane may lift off before it can climb out of ground effect. If you pull it off too early and climb, it can settle back toward the runway. Stay low and accelerate to the climb speed in ground effect, then climb. The Airplane Flying Handbook's soft-field takeoff uses this on purpose.

Margins

Chart numbers come from a new airplane flown by a test pilot. Your airplane's engine may be tired, your technique won't be perfect, and the wind may shift. Many instructors add a margin, such as 50 percent to the chart's total distance, and pick an abort point on the runway: if you're not at a set airspeed by that point, you close the throttle and stop while there's runway left.

Climb

Climb performance comes from excess. Climb angle depends on excess thrust, the thrust left over after overcoming drag. Climb rate depends on excess power. Both shrink as density altitude rises or weight increases.

VX, best angle of climb
Gives the most altitude for the distance covered over the ground. Use it to clear an obstacle right after takeoff.
VY, best rate of climb
Gives the most altitude for the time spent. Use it for a normal climb to get to altitude quickly, and to keep the engine cooler than VX would.

As altitude increases, VX increases and VY decreases. They meet at the airplane's absolute ceiling, where the airplane can no longer climb at all. The service ceiling is lower: it's the altitude where the best rate of climb drops to 100 feet per minute. Your POH gives both speeds, often with a note on how VY changes with altitude.

On a hot day at a high-elevation field, the climb gradient may be so shallow that the airplane can lift off but can't clear terrain beyond the runway. Check the rate-of-climb chart for your density altitude, and compare the climb gradient with the obstacles you'll face.

Cruise, range, and endurance

Cruise charts show true airspeed, fuel flow, and percent of power for combinations of pressure altitude, temperature, and RPM (or manifold pressure). Two goals pull in different directions.

Maximum range
The most distance per gallon. It happens near the speed of best lift-to-drag ratio. Use it when you need to cover distance on the fuel you have.
Maximum endurance
The most time aloft per gallon. It happens at the speed that needs the least power, which is slower than the max-range speed. Use it for holding or waiting out weather.

Climbing higher usually raises true airspeed at the same power setting, because the air is thinner and drag is lower. Normally aspirated engines lose power with altitude, though, so at some point the airplane can't make the power the chart assumes. Wind is the other big factor. A headwind at a higher altitude can cancel out the speed you gain there, so compare groundspeeds at the altitudes you're considering.

Fuel figures in the POH assume the mixture is leaned as the POH describes. Leaning wrong, or not at all, burns more fuel than the chart shows. Plan with the chart, then check actual fuel burn in flight against your plan.

Landing

Landing distance has the same two numbers as takeoff: ground roll, and total distance over a 50-foot obstacle. The same factors apply, with a few differences.

  • Density altitude raises the true airspeed and groundspeed at touchdown, so the roll gets longer.
  • Extra weight raises the approach speed and the energy the brakes have to absorb.
  • A headwind shortens the landing and a tailwind lengthens it a lot.
  • Wet grass, wet pavement, and standing water reduce braking. Hydroplaning can leave you with almost no braking at all.
  • Excess speed on final and a long flare eat runway fast. The chart assumes the approach speed and technique it lists. Every extra knot over the target speed adds distance.

Before you land, know the landing distance you need and the landing distance available. If you're not on speed and on the touchdown point, go around.

Reading the POH charts

Performance data comes as either a table or a graph. The method is the same for both: enter each condition, follow it through, and read the answer. Your airplane's POH Section 5 has its own charts, with notes printed beside them. Read the notes every time. They give the conditions the chart assumes and the corrections for wind, surface, and other factors.

Tables and interpolation

Tables give values at set steps, such as every 1,000 feet of pressure altitude and every 10°C. When your conditions fall between steps, interpolate: find how far between the two rows or columns you are, and take that same fraction of the difference between their values.

Made-up example: total takeoff distance over a 50-foot obstacle at one weight
Pressure altitude20°C30°C
1,000 ft1,600 ft1,720 ft
2,000 ft1,750 ft1,880 ft

These numbers belong to an imaginary airplane. Say pressure altitude is 1,400 feet and the temperature is 30°C. 1,400 is 40 percent of the way from 1,000 to 2,000. In the 30°C column, the difference is 1,880 − 1,720 = 160 feet, and 40 percent of that is 64 feet. So the distance is about 1,720 + 64 = 1,784 feet. If temperature also falls between columns, interpolate in both directions. Many pilots simply round up to the next worse row and column, which is quicker and stays on the safe side.

Graphs

Graph-style charts chain several panels together. A typical takeoff graph starts with temperature, moves up to the pressure altitude line, then across to a weight panel, then a wind panel, then an obstacle panel. In each panel you follow the guide lines that slope through it until you reach your value, then move straight across to the next panel. Use a straightedge and a sharp pencil. A thick line or a drift away from the guide lines can easily cost a hundred feet.

Crosswind component

The crosswind chart turns a wind direction and speed into headwind and crosswind components for your runway. Find the angle between the wind and the runway, follow that angle line out to the wind speed arc, then read straight down for the crosswind component and straight across for the headwind. Compare the crosswind with the maximum demonstrated crosswind velocity in the POH. That figure isn't a limitation. It's the highest crosswind the manufacturer tested, and your instructor will likely set a lower personal limit while you train.

Putting it together

  1. Get the field elevation, altimeter setting, temperature, and wind from the ATIS, AWOS, or METAR.

  2. Work out pressure altitude and density altitude.

  3. Use your actual takeoff weight from your weight and balance.

  4. Read the takeoff ground roll and 50-foot obstacle distance, and apply the chart notes for wind and surface.

  5. Compare with the runway available, add your margin, and choose an abort point.

  6. Do the same for landing at your destination, with the conditions you expect there.
